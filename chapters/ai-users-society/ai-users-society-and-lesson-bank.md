## 0. Purpose

This specification is the engineering part of the essay “Billions in the Shadow” [1]. The essay explains why the contribution of AI users is in the shadow today — made in plain sight, yet not recognized, not counted and not covered by any contract — and why bringing it into the light benefits both sides. Here is how that light can be shed: what a users’ organization does, by what rules, and what a first pilot may and may not claim to have proved.

It rests on one idea: an AI maker and its billions of users are, together, the subject of production. The users already take part in it: their recorded joint work with AI becomes material for training the next models. What is missing is not the participation but its count, its recognition and its rules.

The specification is built in the same order. For decades household work was left out of the national accounts — the consolidated books by which a country reckons how much it has produced; recognition began when that work started to be counted [2]. Hence the order: first the count, then recognition, then rules. Part One is the count: the record of a lesson, and a number that can be checked. Part Two is recognition: the contribution acquires a party that speaks for it, and a contract under which it is accepted and paid for. Part Three is the rules: a procedure for the unforeseen, independence, and the testing of the design itself.

This is a preliminary specification. It describes something that does not yet exist — a seed from which a direction begins. Every item is a testable hypothesis, not a finished solution. What cannot be promised is not promised. The thresholds are working values and are reviewed on schedule (PS-43).

What is not repeated here. The detailed structure of a lesson, and the measurement of its outcome over time, are the subject of the author’s separate working specifications. Who should be admitted to the training of models is covered in “Conditions of Admission” [3]. What a third-generation AI gives a person before a step is covered in “The Map of a Step” [4]. This specification adds what those lack: organization, law and money.

The items are numbered PS-01, PS-02 and so on; the numbering differs from that of edition v1.0. Each requirement is followed by a short note on why it is needed. Where one side takes on an obligation toward another, the item is built like a receipt: what is promised, how to check it, and what is not promised. A promise with no way of checking it does not enter the specification.

## 1. Words as they are used here

- User — a person who works with AI and pays for it with money, time and mind.
- Maker — a company that builds and releases AI models.
- Shadow — participation that goes on in plain sight but is not recognized: nobody counts it, no contract covers it, it has no name, and no party speaks for it. Not a secret, and not a breach of law.
- Organization — an independent society of AI users: it represents its members, keeps the lesson bank and, separately, the application showcase.
- Lesson — a record of the joint work of a person and an AI, together with its outcome: the task with its circumstances, the AI’s move, the person’s correction, the action, and how it all ended.
- Outcome — what actually happened after the AI’s advice, beyond the conversation.
- Full lesson — a lesson with an outcome: status “confirmed” or “participant’s account” (PS-07). Half-lesson — a conversation with corrections but no outcome, status “not known yet”: a blank that awaits the person’s return (PS-08).
- Donor — a member of the organization who gives a lesson to the bank of their own accord.
- Lesson bank — a store of records that carry no proprietary claims: a library of experience, not a showcase.
- Application showcase — a separate section of the organization where an author displays an idea for which a patent application has been filed.
- Backing — a maker’s help to an author in bringing an idea from the showcase to a result: the patent, lawyers, money — in exchange for a share under a rule known in advance.
- Patronage — a maker’s financial support of the organization itself. Not to be confused with the backing of an author: patronage counts toward that maker’s share cap (PS-45).
- Contribution map — a record of who contributed what to the work: the person and each of the AIs. It can serve as the basis of a dispute only if it meets PS-11.
- Complete record — the user’s input, the AI’s visible answer, the user’s action and the outcome the user reported. Not the internal workings of the model (PS-04).
- Release — a model that the maker itself has announced to users under a new name or number. Not an internal draft, a selection run or a test.
- Model card — the document with which a maker announces a release and describes what is new in it.
- Release count sheet — a document the organization publishes before it opens payment for inclusion in a release: what counts as one release, and what evidence proves that a record went into it (PS-31). Until the sheet exists, there is no such payment.
- Held-out lessons — the portion of full lessons that is not shown during training, so that the new model can be tested on it after release (PS-50).

## 2. Three different papers

The one word “lesson” must not be used for three things at once: the trace of the work, the right to train a model on it, and the right to an idea. While they are mixed up, payment for the trace looks like trading in an idea, and acceptance into the bank looks like giving up a patent — and every lawyer will choose the reading that suits their side. So each thing has its own paper, and one paper does not replace another.

**PS-01. The record.** The text of the lesson and the status of its outcome. It is stored, verified and counted, and reports are written from it. By itself the record gives no one a patent and permits no training.

**PS-02. The donor’s training permission.** A separate signature: a named maker, or a class of makers named in advance, may train a model on this record, and the donor will bring no action either over that training or over the operation of a model into which the record entered under the bank’s rules. The signature does not cover someone else’s product that merely resembles the donor’s idea. It is not given after the fact and cannot be replaced by a checkbox.

**PS-03. The proprietary claim.** An exclusive right to an idea, a share of a product’s revenue, or a payment whose size depends on the product’s success. A proprietary claim has no place in the bank; its place is the showcase, and only under Section 9. Contractual payment for the record itself — for participation and for inclusion in a release (PS-30) — is not a proprietary claim.

# Part One. The count: the contribution becomes visible

## 3. The record and the lesson

Light can be shed only on what has been recorded. So the first line is a person’s right to a record of their work with AI, and the second is what a lesson consists of.

**PS-04. The complete record.** *Promised:* the user receives a record of their joint work with the AI — their input, the AI’s visible answer, their action and the outcome they themselves reported — in full, and in a form that can be taken away and passed to someone else. The boundary is fixed in the terms of service. *How to check:* the person compares the record received with what they saw on the screen; a discrepancy is a breach. *Not promised:* the model’s internal states, the maker’s system instructions, safety logs and inferences the AI drew on its own — these are not part of the record, and they do not exist in a form fit to be handed over. The European right to data portability [5] is only a model for the boundary here: it covers only personal data that the person provided or that was observed during use, and the specification is not limited to its territory. *Why:* a demand for “everything on the server” is one the maker will not meet, and with it would fall what can be met.

**PS-05. What a lesson consists of.** The task with its circumstances — for whom, under what conditions, by what date — to the extent that passes PS-21; the AI’s move; the person’s correction; the person’s action; the outcome. A record without an outcome is not yet a lesson but a blank that awaits the person’s return (PS-08). *Why:* the same advice in different circumstances is different advice; the margin note “in our oven, leave it longer” is valuable precisely for its circumstances.

**PS-06. Two mandatory answers.** What the person actually did — in full, halfway or differently — and how it is known how things ended. *Why:* success may have come not from the AI’s advice but from changed circumstances or someone else’s help.

**PS-07. Outcome status.** Every lesson carries one of three statuses: “confirmed,” “participant’s account,” “not known yet.” “Confirmed” is given only on external evidence: a document, a client’s comment, a public link or another trace outside the participant’s own account. A spoken “it worked” is not enough for this status. A lesson with the status “not known yet” is kept, and the bank returns to it later. The shares of the three statuses are part of the bank’s count (PS-12). A status is a label for the recipient, not a cash desk: payment does not depend on it (PS-28). *Why:* honest not-knowing is worth more than a handsome “it all worked”; an invented outcome teaches the AI to be confidently wrong, and it is easier for a group to put together an agreed fiction than for one person to wait for an outcome.

**PS-08. Half a lesson is not passed off as a whole one.** A record without an outcome is marked as a blank and does not serve as evidence that the advice helped. In the bank’s count (PS-12), and in the report on the use of the records received that a maker gives the bank under the transfer agreement (PS-34), half-lessons and full lessons are shown separately, and the status of each record matches its status in the bank: “not known yet” does not turn into “it worked,” whether by a note or by silence. *Why:* an AI that sees a thousand times that its advice was accepted, and never once how things ended, learns to be convincing, not to be right; whoever counts halves as wholes hides this from themselves.

**PS-09. Who asks about the outcome.** When the bank returns to a person for the outcome, it asks in its own name — not in the name of the AI that gave the advice, and not through the service of that AI’s maker. The lesson record notes who asked about the outcome. Outcomes gathered by a maker’s button (PS-48) are accepted by the bank on porting, but kept with that note and shown separately in the count. *Why:* people answer the one who gave the advice politely, not honestly; outcomes should be gathered by a hand to which the person owes no politeness.

**PS-10. Contradictory cases.** Two lessons with different outcomes in a similar task are not merged into one rule: both are kept, together with their circumstances. *Why:* the difference in circumstances may itself be the main knowledge.

**PS-11. The contribution map.** A lesson is accompanied by a record of who contributed what: the person and each of the AIs. The map is admitted into a dispute, or into the choice of a backer, only if it carries machine-readable timestamps and the author’s explicit note of what they did themselves. A record drawn up after the fact and without timestamps is kept as an account but does not serve as the basis of a dispute. The map does not itself compute a fair share and does not replace the list of inventors under patent law; a dispute is settled by the commission under rules known before the work began (PS-14). *Why:* without a trace of time, a contribution cannot be told apart from a rewriting of history.

## 4. A count without names

**PS-12. The bank’s count.** The bank keeps, and for every reporting period publishes, its count without names: how many lessons were accepted; how many of them are full and how many are halves; how many carry each status (PS-07) and how many have an outcome gathered by a maker’s service (PS-09); what share passed a spot re-check (PS-25); how many records were transferred, to how many makers and for what purpose — storage and reports, or training; how many withdrawal requests and porting requests were fulfilled, and how quickly. *Why:* the count comes first — a contribution that has not been counted can be neither recognized nor paid for.

**PS-13. Recognition by number.** *Promised:* under the transfer agreement (PS-34), a maker whose release includes records from the bank states in the model card how many went in — full lessons and halves separately — and from how many donors; it reveals no names. *How to check:* until there is a release count sheet (PS-31), this number is the maker’s own statement: the auditor checks it against the bank’s count, and the records that went in cannot outnumber those transferred to that maker for the purpose “training.” Once the sheet exists, the number is checked against it. *Not promised:* a model cannot give each of its teachers back a name, and a number does not replace one; what exactly each lesson changed in the model is not stated, because nobody knows how to measure it. *Why:* a number in a document is the nearest thing to a name that can be given back, and it can be checked.

# Part Two. Recognition: the contribution acquires a party

## 5. What the organization does

**PS-14. Representation.** The organization negotiates with makers on behalf of its members over the conditions of Section 8, and settles disputes by rules written down in advance — through a commission, not by one voice. *Why:* a single user cannot bargain terms out of a corporation.

**PS-15. A framework, not a price and not a seller.** The organization sets no prices and sells no lessons. It builds the legal ground on which user and maker are linked — as partners, not only as seller and buyer: standard contracts, rules for counting and verification, the settlement of disputes, a procedure for new cases (PS-41). The form of payment is chosen by the parties to a contract: a one-off payment for participation, privileges and — when that form is open — payment for inclusion in a release (PS-31). The organization takes no share of these sums. *Why:* the organization is a link between maker and user, not a data shop.

**PS-16. The lesson bank.** The organization collects its members’ records, verifies them, keeps their count (PS-12), stores them and opens them to makers on the conditions of Sections 6–8. *Why:* a return channel from people to the model, which today barely exists.

**PS-17. Checking promises.** The organization checks makers’ promises against the outcomes gathered in the bank and publishes the comparison. It takes no money for ratings and no advertising. *Why:* that is how the consumer organizations people trust do their work.

**PS-18. The fairness mark.** The organization awards a maker the mark “fair to its users” only if three conditions are met: its service complies with Section 8 and PS-48; over the reporting period, a share of the requests to port a lesson to the independent bank, announced in advance, was fulfilled within the announced time; and on the porting route there was no technical barrier of which the bank had not been warned in advance. What is counted is fulfilled requests, not a share of all users: most people will not want to port their lessons, and the mark must not reward forced porting. An export button in the text of the terms, without live porting, earns no mark. The threshold is published before the period begins. *Why:* to give a maker a reason to be the first to build a good service — and not to award the mark for the description of a button.

## 6. Acceptance, verification, storage

**PS-19. Consent and choice.** A lesson is given only with the donor’s consent, and only the lesson the donor chose themselves. *Why:* a donor is someone who gives of their own accord; what is taken without choice is not accepted into the bank.

**PS-20. Cooling-off period.** After a lesson is handed in there is a short period, announced in advance; the working threshold is fourteen days. Within that period the donor may take the record back entirely, as if it had never been handed in; it is not passed outside, and no training permission (PS-02) is signed. After the period, withdrawal follows PS-24. *Why:* a warning at the bottom of a page is no substitute for time to think again, and a trace cannot later be taken back out of someone else’s copy.

**PS-21. Other people’s information.** If a lesson contains information about third parties — a patient, a student, a client, an employer — the donor’s consent is not enough. Such a lesson is transferred only de-identified or with their consent; otherwise it stays closed. The bank publishes three lists in advance: which lessons are accepted only de-identified, which only with the third parties’ consent, and which are not accepted at all. The bank must be able to turn down useful material. *Why:* without lists named in advance, every refusal looks arbitrary and every acceptance looks like a leak.

**PS-22. The door to the bank: a signature instead of a checkbox.** The bank accepts a record only if the donor has signed a separate sentence: they assert no proprietary claims to this record — no exclusive right, no share of revenue, no payment tied to a product’s success — against the bank or against those who received the record under the bank’s rules, including the operation of a model into which it entered under those rules. This is a contractual undertaking toward those persons — not a waiver of a patent, not an assignment of a patent and not a license to the text. Whoever wants to keep the right to forbid others to use an idea does not hand the record to the bank: its place is the showcase (PS-39) or with the author. There is no middle ground of “the idea is valuable, but let it lie in the bank for now.” The author decides; the bank tells them so plainly at hand-in and does not guess a claim on their behalf. *Why:* the phrase “I want no income” cannot be verified, and ordinary payment for a trace must be declared neither trading in a patent nor a waiver of one.

**PS-23. What acceptance does not do.** Acceptance into a closed bank does not publish the lesson to the world and does not create what patent law calls prior art. An open license, CC BY included, transfers no patent rights and is not a waiver of them [6]. Whoever wants to make an idea free for all publishes it themselves, separately, and bears the patent consequences under the law of their own country. *Why:* a promise that does not exist in law is more dangerous than silence.

**PS-24. Withdrawing a contribution.** Before a lesson is handed in, the donor is told in writing the difference between three actions: deleting the record from the bank; stopping any new transfers of it; removing its trace from a model already trained. *Promised:* the bank performs the first two actions at the donor’s request, and in the contract with the recipient (PS-27) they are binding on the recipient too. *How to check:* the bank’s count (PS-12) shows the number of withdrawal requests and the time taken to fulfil them; the donor is entitled to confirmation that the record has been deleted and that there are no new transfers. *Not promised:* the third action. The bank calls it a best-effort attempt only when the maker has a method of removal and a way to show the member that the trace is gone. Until such a method exists, the donor receives the first two actions and a written refusal of the third — one that is not hidden in a general clause of the contract. *Why:* a promise that cannot be checked is worse than an honest refusal.

**PS-25. Checking authenticity.** Before transfer, the bank separates a participant’s account from a confirmed result under the rule of PS-07. The bank does not promise to verify every lesson. The minimum without which a record does not go to a maker: the status has been assigned by the rule, not at the donor’s wish, and spot re-checking is already under way. The cost of one check is published and is part of the payment for the commissioned stream (PS-29), not of the price of a record. If checking costs more than it is worth, PS-52 applies — not a quiet abolition of checking. *Why:* inventing an outcome can be easier than waiting for one, and a promise to check everything at an unknown price is one the bank will not keep.

**PS-26. The tilt toward successes.** The bank itself commissions the return to unfinished matters and failed outcomes through the commissioned stream (PS-29), rather than waiting only for those who succeeded. What the bank may return to, and when, the person marks at hand-in; the bank does not initiate a return to anything else. A person is still entitled to stay silent: a refusal is not penalized and does not cost them their membership. *Why:* otherwise the bank becomes a collection of success stories; a failure is rarely brought in voluntarily.

**PS-27. Who answers for safekeeping.** At hand-in, the bank and the donor sign an acceptance agreement: what has been accepted, for what purpose — storage and reports, or training as well — and what is closed. The bank answers to the donor for its own safekeeping, for selection, and for not opening the purpose “training” without both permissions (PS-33) and without a contract with the recipient. This also covers the bank’s own tools: an AI that the bank uses to de-identify and sort records receives them only on terms that forbid training on them — otherwise processing would become a transfer into training that bypasses PS-33. Before transfer, the contract with the recipient must contain protection of the record, a ban on using it other than as stated, and the first two withdrawal actions (PS-24). For a copy that already lies with a maker, it is the maker who answers to the donor; the bank answers for having handed over a record without such a contract. Storage follows the law of the country where the bank operates. The bank accepts the law of the donor’s country only if that regime is on its published list of regimes it is able to honor; if it is not on the list, the bank declines the record rather than promise a law it cannot fulfil. A complaint goes to the commission (PS-14); the bank publishes leaks and breaches; the audit (PS-45) covers them too; the members may replace the leadership (PS-46). *Why:* protection with no one answerable for it is only a word.

## 7. Money: three pipes

The organization’s money runs through three pipes, and they must not be mixed: otherwise the organization either becomes a seller of records or loses its independence over one large payment. The first pipe is membership dues; alongside them, grants and makers’ patronage come into the budget, and all of this counts toward the cap (PS-45). The second is payment for the time of the commissioned stream, out of the organization’s budget. The third is contractual payment for a record, from the maker straight to the donor, bypassing the budget.

**PS-28. Not for handing in, and not for success.** The bank does not pay for the mere fact of handing in a lesson and does not pay more for a happy ending. In the commissioned stream, an hour of coming back and recording the outcome costs the same for success, for failure and for an honest “not known yet.” *Why:* where blood was paid for by the unit at donation, it more often carried infection [7]; it is the same with lessons.

**PS-29. Two streams of hand-in.** The donor stream: members give lessons for thanks and privileges — early access to new models, help with their own projects; there is no per-item payment. The commissioned stream: the bank commissions the gathering and checking of experience and pays for time and participation — coming back, recording the outcome, preparing the evidence. The boundary runs along the conditions of participation, not along the label. A commissioned lesson may not be called a “donor” lesson in order to avoid paying for the return. *Why:* without a paid return, the bank sees only those who are pleased to tell of a success.

**PS-30. Contractual payment for a record.** Under a contract between maker and donor, a record may be paid for once — for participation — and, when that form is open (PS-31), for each inclusion in a release. The maker pays this to the donor directly. The organization does not pass it through its own hands: it does not enter the organization’s budget, pays for none of the organization’s decisions and does not count toward the maker’s share under PS-45. The one-off payment for participation is paid by the maker with whom it was agreed; how many times it may be repeated with another maker is written in the contract — by default, once. *Why:* otherwise either the organization trades in records, or a single contract breaks through the independence cap.

**PS-31. Payment for inclusion in a release: closed for now.** Payment for each inclusion of a verified record in the release of a new model — once per release — is not offered in contracts until the organization has published a release count sheet: what counts as one release, and what document an independent auditor accepts as proof of inclusion without seeing the rest of the training set. No sheet, no such form; the one-off payment, privileges and payment for time apply. When the form opens, the rules are these: the payer is the maker that released the model and into whose release the record went; the permission of the maker whose AI conducted the conversation neither cancels this payment nor shifts it onto anyone else; affiliated companies may not fold different releases into one; nothing is paid for a draft, a selection run or a test — no release, no payment; a record of any of the three statuses is paid for, provided the status was assigned by the rule, and the amount does not depend on the outcome and is no higher for “confirmed”; the influence of a record on the model is neither measured nor paid for; a standard contract that lacks the auditor’s right to such a check does not contain this form. A successful pilot does not replace the sheet and does not open the form. *Why:* inclusion can be counted — the way one counts how many times a song was played on air — but only once it is clear what to count and by what document; a rule that cannot be checked is worth no more than a promise to take a trace out of a model.

**PS-32. What is paid for.** For participation and for coming back; when the form of PS-31 is open, for inclusion in a release as well. Not for influence on the model, which nobody today knows how to measure.

## 8. Transfer, and the contract with makers

**PS-33. Two permissions, both required.** *Promised:* a record goes into training only if there is the donor’s permission (PS-02) and the written permission of the maker whose AI took part in the lesson — for this lesson, or for a class of lessons named in advance. If the person worked with several AIs, the permission of every maker whose answers are in the transferred record is needed. At hand-in the donor names the purpose themselves: storage and reports, or training as well; the bank does not fill in the second purpose for them. *How to check:* the numbers of both permissions are kept with every transferred record, and an auditor can verify them. *Not promised:* that the maker’s permission will be obtained — securing it is the task of representation (PS-14). Terms of use often forbid training competing models on an AI’s answers [8], and one permission does not replace the other; without the maker’s permission a record serves reports and the checking of promises (PS-17) and is not transferred for training. *Why:* otherwise the very first maker will cut off the members’ access, and the bank will become a channel for getting around other parties’ terms.

**PS-34. How records are transferred.** *Promised:* transfer takes place under a contract, with a note of provenance, of the outcome status (PS-07) and of who asked about the outcome (PS-09); the recipient sees whose information this is and what it does not cover. Under the same contract the recipient reports to the bank how the records were used — storage and reports, or training, and which release they went into — with a separate count of halves and full lessons (PS-08) and with the number for the model card (PS-13). *How to check:* the recipient’s report is checked against the bank’s count (PS-12); the status of a record in the report and in the bank must match, and a discrepancy is a breach of contract. *Not promised:* that the bank will see the rest of the recipient’s training set — the report concerns only the transferred records. *Why:* a recipient that does not report sends the records back into the shadow.

**PS-35. A common bank.** A user is entitled to pass their lessons to a bank open to different makers. The right to put a record in the bank is not yet the right to train someone else’s model on it: that is decided by PS-33.

**PS-36. A share of success — only for support, and not in this pilot.** A maker’s share of an author’s commercial success is possible only in exchange for backing, and only under Section 9. In the first pilot no such share is signed, and the organization does not tell its members that backing already works.

**PS-37. An unfiled idea in a conversation.** An ordinary conversation with an AI is not a non-disclosure agreement. Protection switches on only if the idea was marked as unfiled — no patent application yet — before the conversation, and the maker accepted that regime in writing: then it does not train on the idea and does not disclose it. A mark added after the fact does not switch the regime on. Without an accepted regime the advice stands: first the application to the patent office, then the conversation. Whether a conversation counts as a disclosure after which a patent can no longer be obtained, the specification does not decide: the answer differs from country to country, and this is a matter for a lawyer.

**PS-38. No exclusivity.** The contract contains no clauses of the kind “since you used our AI, the first right is ours.” The backer is chosen by the author (PS-40).

## 9. The application showcase and backing

The rules of this section are not part of the first pilot (PS-49) and are not confirmed by it. They are tested separately, where there is a commercial outcome. Until then, a maker does not sign a share of future success, and the organization does not promise its members that backing already works.

**PS-39. The application showcase.** An idea with a proprietary claim (PS-03) goes not into the bank but onto the showcase — and only after a patent application has been filed whose claims can be read: the application has been published, or the author has given its text to the organization. An inexpensive provisional application will do as well, if its text has been handed over. The organization neither receives applications nor grants patents: it checks that the application is live, who the applicant is, and that the text on the showcase is no broader than the patent claims. The card shows the author, the substance within the scope of the patent claims, the application number and the contribution map, if the latter meets PS-11. The card is taken down when the application has lapsed, been withdrawn or been rejected, or when the text has diverged from the patent claims. The showcase is subject to the same separation of roles as the transfer of records (PS-47). *Why:* income from sales and payment for participation are different economies; an unpublished application cannot be checked against the showcase, and a lapsed card is a false sign.

**PS-40. The backer, and a rule known in advance.** A backing maker takes the application through to a full patent at its own expense, provides lawyers and helps find a buyer — like a technology transfer office at American universities after the 1980 law [9, 10]. In return it receives a share of the income under a rule signed before the work begins: a percentage and a threshold. This specification gives no figure. Other people’s customs — a university inventor’s share, a game “engine’s” percentage above a revenue threshold [11] — are not carried over here as a norm: there the owner of the right is a different party. There is no backing contract until four things are named in it in writing: who the applicant is and who owns the application; what happens if the author abandons the application or the backer withdraws; that the share is also calculated on sales to the backer’s own and affiliated parties, not only on open revenue; and that a narrowing of the patent claims does not tie the invention to the backer’s product. If the idea arose in work with several AIs, the author chooses the backer themselves — by the terms offered and by the contribution map (PS-11); a map without timestamps is no basis for the choice. *Why:* what sets the swing going is a percentage invented after the fact, and a contract with no owner of the application is not a contract.

# Part Three. Rules: how order is kept

## 10. A new case, and review

**PS-41. A case not in the rules.** Not everything can be foreseen: cases will arise for which today there is neither law nor even words. Such a case is examined by the organization’s commission — like a case conference in a hospital when a patient does not fit the protocol. The maker is heard; it has neither a deciding vote nor a veto, and its refusal to talk does not halt the matter. If a rule for the case already exists, the commission briefly dismisses the request — it is not a new case. The time limit for a decision is published in advance; the working threshold is sixty days. The decision is provisional: it is recorded together with the circumstances and published as a precedent, and at the next review (PS-43) it is confirmed, amended or revoked. If the time limit is missed, the safe rule applies, and the question still goes to review. The safe rule is this: no new transfers of the record in question for training are begun; payment for gathering and return work already done does not stop; no payment arises for a transfer that did not take place. The rule concerns one record, not the whole bank, and sets no prices, shares or backing. A ruling involving one maker does not bind another until that maker adopts it into its own contract. Contracts with makers include a clause on good-faith negotiation upon a material change of circumstances — such clauses are known to the law of long-term contracts [12]; it brings the parties to the table but does not replace the safe rule. *Why:* the unforeseen must have a procedure; an impasse must not become a veto, and a veto must not write the share of success.

**PS-42. A long-term contract.** Contracts with makers are concluded for a long term, with the same participants. A long contract alone is not enough: long contracts alone do not damp the cycle [13]. It works only together with PS-28 and PS-43. The first pilot neither confirms nor refutes this item.

**PS-43. Scheduled review.** The rules are reviewed by users and makers together, on schedule; the working interval is every six months. The yardstick is the outcomes the bank has managed to verify, and the result of the test on held-out lessons (PS-50). The thresholds themselves are reviewed too: the share cap (PS-45), the threshold for the mark (PS-18), the cooling-off period (PS-20), the commission’s time limit (PS-41) and, once it exists, the release count sheet (PS-31).

## 11. Independence

**PS-44. Dues.** The membership fee may be withheld from the subscription payment — a dollar, for example. It is the user’s money: the maker merely passes it on, as a payroll office passes on union dues. Forwarded dues nevertheless count toward that maker’s share (PS-45). Free users join without dues and with an equal vote: their contribution is mind. Membership is voluntary; there may be several organizations, and each speaks only for its own members.

**PS-45. The cap.** The organization’s money comes from several makers and from the users themselves. The share of one maker, together with its affiliated companies, may not be the larger part of the budget and — until the charter names a different threshold after the first year — does not exceed a quarter of the year’s income. Counted toward that quarter is everything that came to the organization from the maker and its affiliates: forwarded dues, patronage, grants, payment for the commissioned stream if the maker provided it, deals on the showcase, and anything else. Not counted is contractual payment for a record, which goes to the donor past the organization (PS-30). An independent auditor checks the sources and which decisions they paid for; the members see the report. A quarter is a working cap, not a measured quantity. *Why:* the formula “not the larger part” is circumvented by splitting and by “voluntary” grants, and a payment to a donor must not be booked as support of the organization.

**PS-46. Governance.** The organization is governed by its members: they elect the leadership, may replace it and may limit its mandate. Makers may have a seat at the table, but neither a deciding vote nor a veto. A seat at the table gives no access to donors’ personal data or to closed lessons. *Why:* in the United States, unions dominated by the employer have been banned since 1935 [14]; a representation that one maker both feeds and decides for is that maker’s own service, whatever it is called.

**PS-47. Separation of roles.** Whoever is responsible for income from the transfer of records and from deals on the showcase does not decide complaints against a large buyer alone. The members see where the money came from and which decisions it paid for.

## 12. The lower floor: services under the makers’ roofs

**PS-48. Permissible and useful.** A maker may build its own user-protection service and build in a button: “come back and tell us how it ended.” *Promised* (by a maker that wants the fairness mark, PS-18): the user can port their lesson from that service to the independent bank, and porting requests during the reporting period are actually fulfilled. *How to check:* the share of porting requests fulfilled and the time taken, as published by the bank; a description of porting in the service’s terms earns no mark. *Not promised:* that outcomes gathered by such a button are equal to those gathered by the bank — on porting they keep the note of who asked (PS-09) and are shown separately in the count. *Why:* a service under a maker’s roof is useful, but it cannot bargain with the one who pays it (PS-46).

## 13. Testing the design

**PS-49. The pilot, and what it does not prove.** One community where the result can be verified — for example, translators willing to show the original task, the edits and the client’s comments, with permission to transfer them — and one maker willing to try. Before the start, not after a successful report, it is stated what this pilot does not test and what it may not be cited for: the showcase, backing and the share of success (Section 9); the third withdrawal action (PS-24); the multi-year independence of the money, and the cap after the first year (PS-45); the long-term contract as a way of damping the swing (PS-42); the list of the legal regimes of different countries that the bank is able to honor, if the pilot runs in one country (PS-27); payment for inclusion — the form is closed, and the pilot does not open it (PS-31); the commission’s time limit, and the force of its ruling for another maker (PS-41); the proprietary-claim door (PS-22) — the untroubled lessons of translators do not prove that it will hold a contested idea; the return for an outcome that arrives months or years later — translators see their outcome within days, while the bank is needed most where the outcome comes late. *Why:* a narrow pilot is easily taken for proof of the whole design.

**PS-50. Testing on held-out lessons.** Whether lessons help is tested the way a student is tested — on problems the student has not seen. *Promised:* a maker that takes the bank’s full lessons for training gives the bank, before training begins, the numbers of the held-out portion, and does not show that portion during training. After release it tests the new model and the previous one on those lessons — for example, whether the model can tell how things ended — and publishes the result before announcing that the lessons helped. A poor result is published in the same way as a good one. *How to check:* the share of held-out lessons and the method of comparison are named before training and not changed afterwards; the list of numbers is given to the bank before training begins; the result is published before success is claimed. *Not promised:* that the result will be good; if it is not, the lessons did not help, and that too is an outcome (PS-52). *Why:* without a held-out portion, the answer to “did it help?” comes from the parties’ confidence, not from a test.

**PS-51. Four questions.** The first pilot answers them. Can lessons be gathered without undue effort? What does it cost to check one lesson? Do lessons with outcomes help to train or test models better — the answer comes from the test of PS-50. Is there written permission for transfer into training (PS-33), and are the first two withdrawal actions (PS-24) carried out? The fourth question is answered “yes” or “no” from documents, not from the parties’ intentions. Four “yes” answers say nothing about the list in PS-49.

**PS-52. If it is not confirmed.** If lessons with outcomes do not help, or checking costs more than it is worth, the design of the bank is changed. Representation (PS-14) remains: it has a basis of its own. A negative answer to the fourth question halts transfer into training, but does not close representation and does not cancel storage for reports.

## 14. What earlier collections teach

This section is not a set of rules but the experience of those who have already gathered outcomes and lessons, or paid for an outcome: what worked, what did not, and why. It is meant for those who will build such an organization in practice.

**The surgeon Codman, Boston, 1910–1918.** He proposed that a hospital should follow every patient until it was clear whether the treatment had helped and, if it had not, find out why. His colleagues did not support him, so he opened his own hospital: he followed every discharged patient and published the outcome of each at his own expense, failures included. He then publicly reproached his colleagues for caring more about money than about outcomes — and was left with almost no patients and no income. The idea survived when an institution took it up: the American College of Surgeons committee on hospital standardization, which he chaired, eventually led to the commission that accredits US hospitals, and his bone sarcoma registry became the forerunner of cancer registries [15]. *What it teaches:* an outcome collection that rests on one person and his purse, and puts colleagues’ outcomes on display, meets resistance from those being judged; it survives once it becomes an institution’s rule.

**US aviation, 1975–1976.** The regulator, which itself investigates and punishes, opened a channel for incident reports — and in six months received not one. Collection was then handed to NASA, a party kept separate from investigation and punishment. The principles: voluntary, confidential, non-punitive, independent. The regulator pays for the system, and a federal rule forbids it to use the reports in enforcement; a person who reports their own violation within ten days is, under certain conditions, spared the penalty. NASA cuts off the top strip of the form, which carries the name, stamps it with the date and returns it to the author as a receipt, keeping no record of the name; the rest of the text is cleared of everything that points to the author or to the people named in it. The exceptions are stated in advance: crimes and accidents. The system does not only store reports but also sends out alerts. In 45 years it received more than 1.8 million reports, now about 100,000 a year [16]. *What it teaches:* people report their own errors to someone who does not judge, and only under a protection known in advance; the best protection is not to keep anything that could point to the person.

**NASA’s lesson bank, reviews of 2002 and 2012.** Project managers were required to enter lessons into a shared database and to consult it. The 2002 review found that only 23% of the managers surveyed had ever entered anything; they found the database hard to use and could not find useful lessons in it, and before the loss of the shuttle Columbia in 2003 it was hardly ever consulted. The 2012 review found the same [17]. *What it teaches:* an obligation to hand in lessons does not make them needed; a bank from which lessons do not return to where decisions are made becomes an archive.

**Paying for the outcome: aircraft engines and AI customer support.** Since 1962 an aircraft engine can be paid for by the flying hour: the customer pays only for an engine that works, and the two sides’ interests converge; in time the maker itself began to monitor engines in flight through onboard sensors [18]. With AI this began in customer support: vendors charge for each request the AI resolves on its own. But a “resolution” is also counted when the customer simply leaves without asking for more help [19]: the outcome is measured at the edge of the conversation, and it is counted by the party that is paid for it. *What it teaches:* an outcome starts to be measured once revenue depends on it — but it is measured where measuring is cheap. So what counts as an outcome is written down before any payment (PS-07, PS-31), and the outcome is counted by someone other than the party paid for it (PS-09, PS-28). The bank would do well to look for its first partner among makers whose revenue already depends on outcomes.

**Five conditions, and where they stand in this specification.**

- The one who collects does not judge: PS-09, PS-46, PS-48.
- The outcome is counted by someone other than the party paid for it, and the person who reports it earns no more for a success: PS-07, PS-09, PS-28.
- The collection rests on an institution and a payer, not on one enthusiast: Section 7, PS-45.
- Lessons return to where decisions are made: PS-17, PS-34, PS-50.
- The person who reports their own error is protected. Here the specification is weakest: PS-21 protects third parties but not the donor, and PS-26 admits that failures are rarely brought in voluntarily. The question is listed among the open ones (Section 15).

## 15. Open questions

- How a record’s contribution to a model might one day be measured, and whether the rule of PS-32 should then change.
- What method of removing a trace from a model should be accepted as sufficient even to discuss the third action of PS-24. Until there is a criterion, the action is not promised.
- What release count sheet to publish before opening payment for inclusion (PS-31) and before checking recognition by number (PS-13) against a document rather than a statement. Until there is a sheet, there is no such form.
- How to publish the test on held-out lessons (PS-50) without revealing the lessons themselves or the people behind them.
- What level of checking pays for itself. The minimum is set — status under PS-07 and spot re-checking (PS-25); the price and sufficiency of that minimum are measured by the pilot (PS-51).
- How to tell a correction on the merits from a correction of taste while the outcome is unknown. Today there is no way; that is why the outcome is needed.
- Whether, over time, the comparison of promises (PS-17) could yield what general-purpose AI lacks today — instructions for use with indications, like a medicine’s: where AI advice has been checked against outcomes, where only in part, and where not at all.
- How to protect a donor who reports their own error. The most valuable lesson reads: “The AI advised, I acted, it went badly”; for a doctor, lawyer or engineer this is in effect testimony against oneself, and without protection such lessons will not come (PS-26). Aviation solved this with a rule that forbids the regulator to use reports in enforcement, and with a collector that keeps no record of the author’s name (Section 14). The lesson bank has no such rule and cannot by itself set aside a court order. One possible design is a receipt mode: after checking, the bank removes from the record everything that points to the donor and gives the donor a receipt with the record’s number; only the donor holds the link between record and person — with the receipt the donor can withdraw the record (PS-24) or confirm their participation, while the bank has nothing to hand over. The price of this mode: direct payment from a maker (PS-30) and the contribution map (PS-11) need a link to the person, and in this mode they probably do not exist. Unresolved: the content of a lesson may itself point to the person; which exceptions to name in advance, as aviation did; who could give donors a protection like aviation’s, and how to reconcile it with the law of different countries.
- What cap on one maker’s share (PS-45) and what threshold of fulfilled porting requests (PS-18) to fix in the charter after the first year.
- How organizations of different countries and professions are to agree among themselves, and how to reconcile different countries’ rules on data, authorship and the disclosure of ideas.
- How to keep the “proprietary claim or not” door (PS-22) from becoming a loophole in both directions: so that a donor does not give away something valuable out of ignorance, and so that ordinary experience is not carried onto the showcase. The cooling-off period and readable patent claims narrow the loophole but do not close it.
- Whether a conversation with an AI counts as a disclosure of an idea after which it can no longer be patented.
- Whether people really report failures to an independent collector more often than to the maker’s button. The test is to compare, on similar cases, outcomes collected by the bank and by the button; PS-09 keeps them separate for this.
- What measure to use when comparing models on held-out lessons (PS-50). Guessing an outcome is not the same as giving advice that improves it: a measure of the advice itself is needed, and the cleanest is a comparison of one and the same model trained with and without the lessons.

## 16. How the specification has changed

The first edition (v0.1) separated the engineering part from the essay. Reviews by three AIs found contradictions and gaps in it, and editions v0.2–v1.0 closed them: the record, the training permission and the proprietary claim were separated as three papers; the bank accepts only experience without proprietary claims, while ideas with a claim go to the showcase with readable patent claims; neither acceptance into the bank nor an open license counts as a waiver of a patent; payment to the donor bypasses the organization’s budget, and payment for inclusion in a release is closed until there is a sheet against which it can be checked; a new case is decided by the organization’s commission — without a maker’s veto, and within a time limit; the bank does not promise to take a trace out of a trained model, and does not promise the law of any donor’s country; the list of what the first pilot does not prove is stated before the start.

Edition v1.1 is aligned with the consolidated essay. The sections are arranged in the order “count, recognition, rules,” and the count has become a separate duty of the bank (PS-12). From the specifications that Claude and Mistral wrote from the AI’s point of view, four rules were taken — half a lesson is not passed off as a whole one (PS-08); the outcome is asked about by someone other than the one who gave the advice (PS-09); recognition by number in the model card (PS-13); testing on held-out lessons (PS-50) — and the receipt form for items in which one side takes on an obligation toward another: what is promised, how to check it, what is not promised (PS-04, PS-13, PS-24, PS-33, PS-34, PS-48, PS-50). Also added: circumstances as part of a lesson, the permission of every maker whose answers are in the record, the distinction between patronage of the organization and backing of an author, and the definition of the release count sheet. In November 2026, before publication, the following were added: the lessons of earlier collections (Section 14), the late outcome in the list of PS-49, and two open questions — on instructions for AI and on protecting the donor; Section 14 also took in the experience of paying for outcomes. At the same time PS-26 (what the bank may return to) and PS-27 (the bank’s own tools) were clarified. After an outside review of the essay, two more open questions were added: whether independent collection of outcomes is more honest, and what measure to use when comparing models on held-out lessons.
