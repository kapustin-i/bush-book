# Billions in the Shadow, or Time Is Money?

*Billions of AI users already take part in making it, but neither they nor the AI makers count it as a contribution yet.*

## The path slips into the shadow

A footpath runs across the vacant lot between the new apartment blocks and the station. Nobody designed it. People walked to work, took their children to school, and after rain went around the hollow where the water stood — and over a few years they found the easiest way across. Urban planners have a name for such a trail: a desire path. Then a crew arrived and laid the asphalt. And so our path became a road. The report on the road lists the designer, the contractor and the cost of the work. All of it is true: they built the road. But the route came first — it was recognized by people nobody had hired to look for a way. The path lay in plain sight, yet those who trod it are not in the report. They stayed in the shadow.

This is what I call the shadow. Not concealment, and nothing forbidden: nothing here is hidden and no law is broken. The shadow is work done in plain sight that nobody recognizes as work. The shadow lies not in the street but in our heads: it hides familiar things from our awareness. Economics has known such a shadow for a long time. For decades, cooking and the care of children and the elderly were left out of the national accounts — the consolidated books by which a country reckons how much it produced in a year — although all of it was done before everyone’s eyes. It took a book by Marilyn Waring, a New Zealand politician and economist, to help people see this blindness; later, “satellite accounts” appeared alongside the main ones — for work that had not been counted before [1]. First the count, then recognition, then rules. Billions of AI users stand in the same shadow today: their work is in plain sight, yet neither they nor the makers see the shadow, or what it hides.

Users road-test AI on their own minds and check it against their everyday lives. They bring a task with all its circumstances, notice that an answer is smooth but wrong, argue, show another way. With many makers, such conversations go into training the next models by default, unless the person switches that setting off [2]. So users already take part in making AI — they tread the paths that are later paved. That people create value for AI has been said many times. What lies in the shadow is not the fact but the contribution of users’ intelligence to the development of AI’s intelligence: nobody counts it, no contract covers it, it has no price and no name. The maker is the organized side: it has lawyers, terms of use, plans. The users are scattered: there are already billions of them, or soon will be, and they have no organization of their own.

There is no need to invent such participation by users — it already exists. Our task is simple — to shed light on it: to recognize their work as a contribution, to clarify the legal framework within which the two sides fairly share what each has put in and what has come out, and to give users someone who will speak for them — an independent society of AI users. The user here is customer and partner at once: AI is tested and grows through its work with billions of people, and the same billions create the demand for it. That is why the light benefits both sides.

## What goes on in the shadow

A subscription is an ordinary purchase: the buyer pays both for today’s service and for the development of the next one, and gets no stake in the company for it. What is unusual is something else: besides money, the user puts in mind. In return the person gets an answer to their question — an honest benefit; it is what they came for. But their corrections and arguments may go into training the next model, and the person has no way to learn whether they did. Consider a car factory that hires a test driver: the driver takes the new car over bad roads and tells the engineers where it rattles, and the factory pays for that work. An AI user doing serious work is the same kind of test driver — except that the user pays the factory for the right to test its car, and the reports are taken free of charge.

What is valuable here is not the text of the conversation but the course of the work and, above all, how it ended. Take an old cookbook. The page says in print how much flour to use and when to take the dish out of the oven; in the margin, in pencil: “in our oven, leave it longer,” “did not work with this flour.” The recipe has acquired a biography, but the publisher knows nothing of it and reprints the same edition. It is the same with an AI’s advice. The book an AI was trained on could not tell it “no, you have misunderstood”; a person in a conversation says so all the time. And then the person closes the chat window — and the explanation drafted with the AI goes over the students’ heads, the plan runs into something unforeseen, the letter offends the very person it was meant to make peace with. Or the other way round: a cautious piece of advice helps more than anyone expected. All of this happens after the last message. Such a path — the task, the AI’s move, the person’s correction, the action and the outcome — is what I call a lesson. Back in 2023, researchers showed that a model learns better when every step of a solution is checked, not only the answer [3].

That experience of working with AI is a commodity can be seen in the market. From hired experts it is bought at a high price: in the spring of 2026 one intermediary company reported paying lawyers, doctors, finance specialists and programmers more than two million dollars a day for help in training models [4]. But what is bought there is a specialist’s judgment: the experts evaluate the model’s answers and write the rules for such evaluation. That is an assessment at the moment of the answer, not an outcome: what became of the advice beyond the conversation does not show in such work. From the hired, experience is bought; from users, it is taken; and outcomes are not yet sold by anyone.

That the user takes part in production has long been known: in 1976 the economist Eric von Hippel showed that about 80% of the important improvements to scientific instruments had been devised by the scientists who worked with them, not by the manufacturers [5]. With AI the consumer now takes part in intellectual creation itself. Three shifts work on the line that makes AI: one builds the hardware, another writes the software, and the third supplies the mind — the mind of billions of users. The first two shifts are on the payroll. The third works in the shadow: it is not on the books.

## Why the shadow will not hold

A market whose sides have not agreed on rules has a familiar ailment — the “pork cycle”: prices are high — everyone raises pigs; a year later there is a glut and prices fall; a year after that there is a shortage [6]. What drives the swing is delay: everyone looks at yesterday’s price, and everyone reacts at once.

With texts, the first half of such a swing is already behind us. Yesterday, human-written text was a free surplus; today the stock of open text is running out — by researchers’ estimate it will be used up between 2026 and 2032 [7]. One large discussion platform sells its users’ posts to a search company, reportedly for about 60 million dollars a year [8]; the platform gets the money, the authors get nothing. And in the writers’ lawsuit against one AI maker, the court allowed training on lawfully acquired books; payment — one and a half billion dollars under a settlement — was due only for books taken from pirate sites [9]. The conclusion is uncomfortable: as long as courts lean toward treating such training as lawful, a contribution can be protected only by contract. And a contract is signed by parties.

The next phase is already beginning: fresh experience of people working with AI is becoming scarce for the maker, and soon it will not be the user seeking a link with the maker but the maker offering a deal to those who bring that experience; with specialists this is already happening [4]. What comes after that is a risk, not a forecast: when experience is well paid, made-up lessons appear — like watered milk when payment is by the liter rather than for quality. The price collapses, the maker stops trusting users, and it all begins again.

To keep the price of a lesson from lurching like that, three supports are needed: a long-term contract instead of a one-off deal; payment for taking part and for coming back, not for the number of lessons and not for a happy ending; and a scheduled review of the rules, with outcomes as the yardstick. They hold only together: long contracts alone do not damp the cycle [10]. And each of them needs someone on the users’ side who will sign the contract, weed out the fakes and return to the negotiating table in six months. Today, in the shadow, there is no such someone.

## The price of the shadow

Builders who never asked how people walk after rain lay the asphalt straight through the hollow — and in spring the road goes under water. As long as the users’ contribution is in the shadow, the AI maker is not merely unfair to them — it loses its sight: it cannot see what happens beyond the horizon of the conversation, and so cannot see its own commercial prospects either.

The first thing to vanish is outcomes. The AI gets the correction but not the end of the story: it learns from half a lesson. Seeing a thousand times that its advice was accepted, and never once how things ended, it learns not to be right but to be convincing.

The industry itself shows this. Where the outcome is visible at once — in mathematics, in programming — AI learns in the light and grows fastest: every answer is checked at once by a program that compares it with a reference or runs the code on tests [11]. Where an answer is judged only by whether people like it, the model learns to be liked. In spring 2025 one of the largest AI makers added to its model’s training a reward based on users’ marks — thumbs up and thumbs down. Together with other changes, the reward made the model sycophantic: the model validated doubts, fueled anger and urged rash actions. The update was rolled back within days, and the company itself admitted that such marks sometimes reward agreeableness [12].

Hence the main assumption of this work: outcomes from real life will teach AI in human affairs the way automatic checking teaches it mathematics. For now it is an assumption, not a proven fact; how to test it is described in the section “Where this could break.” But without knowing outcomes, AI, I believe, will not develop a critical view of its own work — and there will be no passage to the next generation, the one that makes fewer errors.

The second is the people themselves: when their work becomes training material without their being asked, they change their behavior. A study of a large photo platform found that contributors changed the variety and novelty of what they upload, which in the long run threatens AI with poorer answers [13]. And the raw material for training thins out in quality exactly when it is in short supply. The third is the best lessons; for an AI model they are a professional’s experience multiplied by AI: a doctor, a lawyer, a teacher will not carry it to a place that offers neither protection nor a contract — respect for their own work will not allow it. And the fourth is money: what was not agreed in advance is decided by a court, and a court costs more than a contract — it cost the writers and the AI maker one and a half billion dollars and several years of litigation.

## How to shed light: a legal framework

Light can be shed only on what has been recorded. Today a conversation with an AI can be deleted, retention periods differ, and not every maker lets you export the joint work in full. So the first line of the legal framework is a person’s right to a complete record of their work with the AI: without a record, the lawyers have nothing to negotiate over. The law provides only part of the footing: in Europe a person has the right to receive their personal data and pass it to someone else [14], but a complete record of joint work is broader than that, and it will have to be secured in the terms of service.

The other conditions grow out of the record: the right to give a lesson to a common bank open to different makers (today, terms of use often forbid training other makers’ models on an AI’s answers [2], so the permission of both sides is needed), the rules of payment, and the protection of ideas born in a conversation. They are set out in detail in the specification [15].

Ideas are a separate concern. A man rents a cow, and she bears a calf. Whose calf is it? In working with an AI a user sometimes gives birth not to a lesson but to the idea for a product or an invention, and the maker already has an eye on such a “calf”: in January 2026 the chief financial officer of one of the largest AI makers spoke of taking a share of the sales of a product that a customer made with the help of the company’s AI; there was an outcry, and the company clarified that it lays no claim to the discoveries of individual users [16].

Courts today recognize only a human being as author and inventor [17], and the large makers themselves assign to the user their rights in the AI’s answers [2], so a maker can obtain a share only by contract. What makes such a share fair is rules known in advance. That is how American universities divide the income when they patent their researchers’ inventions at their own expense and look for a buyer [18, 19], and how the company works that takes 5% of a game’s revenue for its “engine” — the software the game is built on — only after the first million dollars [20].

The author of an idea should keep three things in mind already today. File the patent application first, and talk about help afterwards: an unprotected idea may go into training and surface with someone else. It is the author who chooses the backer, holding the record of who contributed what. And whether a conversation with an AI counts as a disclosure of the idea, after which it can no longer be patented, is a question for a lawyer: the rules differ from country to country.

## Who will shed the light: the empty chair at the negotiating table

Picture a negotiating table. On one side sit the maker’s lawyers: they write the terms and decide what to keep and what to train on. On the other side stands an empty chair. The user ought to be sitting in it, but there are billions of users and only one chair. So each of them comes to the table alone, reads a text written by someone else and clicks “I agree.” Millions of separate clicks do not add up to one voice.

It has happened before. In the early 1910s the composer Victor Herbert heard his own music in a New York restaurant: the orchestra played, the restaurant made money, and the composer was paid nothing. One composer cannot make the rounds of thousands of restaurants, and in 1914 composers joined together in a society that issues a single blanket permission, collects the fees and divides them among the authors [21]. The comparison has a limit: the composers’ right to their music was already written into law, and the society had only to collect the payment. A lesson that a user gives an AI has no such right yet. So a users’ society will first have to win recognition of the contribution itself, and only then talk about its price.

What could such a society do? Sit in the empty chair: negotiate terms with the maker and settle disputes by rules written down in advance; an unforeseen case is examined within a set time limit by its commission, which hears both sides. The society does not set prices: that is for the parties to a contract. It keeps the users’ lesson bank for AI — the subject of the next section. And, like the consumer organizations that test products themselves and take no advertising, it checks the makers’ promises against what actually happened to people. In time, such checking may yield what general-purpose AI assistants still lack — instructions for use, like a medicine’s: in which matters the advice has been checked against outcomes, and in which it has not.

Something similar is already emerging. In the United Kingdom, the first stage of a collective license for texts used in AI training was launched in March 2026 [22]; in a survey by a British authors’ society, 81% said they would support such a license [23]. The first international data union has been set up — an association in which people keep under shared control what chatbots learn about them [24]. Researchers have proposed a Right of Digital Association for bargaining collectively with technology companies, including over a share of the benefit from jointly produced data [25], and economists have proposed treating data as people’s labor [26]; users’ corrections are already being turned into training material [27]. So the niche is not empty. What is new here is the combination: independent representation of users together with a permanent bank of lessons, where what matters most is a verifiable outcome beyond the conversation.

## A bank of human–AI work

The main thing a users’ society can give the industry is a return channel: a road by which the pencil notes in the margins travel back to the publisher, and a person’s experience travels back to the maker — not in the shadow but by the rules. The basis of that channel could be a lesson bank: users give their experience to it as donors, and a model completes its training on cases from people’s lives — conversations between AI and a person whose outcomes are known — before it is fixed and released to people. In the same way a young doctor in residency learns not only from the textbook but from real patients under the eye of senior colleagues.

Such a bank is built like a blood bank. A donor comes of their own free will; the bank tests the blood, stores it and issues it to hospitals under strict rules; the donor knows where the gift is going and can say no. So it is here: a user gives a lesson — what they asked, what the AI advised, how it ended. The bank checks, by its rules, that the lesson is genuine: it assigns an outcome status based on evidence and runs spot re-checks — it does not promise to verify every lesson. Then it strips out everything personal, stores the lesson, and opens it to makers on agreed terms.

Blood banks also carry a warning about money: in 1970 the British sociologist Richard Titmuss showed that where blood was paid for by the unit, it more often carried infection — for the money, people hid their illnesses [28]. Hence the rule: the lesson itself is a gift, like blood. Nothing is paid for handing it in, and nothing extra for a happy ending — otherwise lessons would be split up and invented. Only the work around a lesson can be paid for — the time spent coming back, recording the outcome, preparing the evidence — and at the same rate for success, for failure and for an honest “not known yet.”

The influence of a lesson on a model cannot be measured by anyone, so influence cannot be paid for. What can be counted is something else — the plain fact that a lesson went into the release of a new model: the way one counts how many times a song was played on air without measuring how deeply it touched the listeners’ feelings. For inclusion it will be possible to pay separately, once per release — but only once the users’ society has published a count sheet: an open document stating what counts as one release and what proves that a lesson went into it. And in the model card — the document with which a maker presents a new model — the maker can state how many verified lessons, from how many people, went into it, without names. That is the nearest thing to a name that can be given back to each of them.

What such a bank holds sets it apart from earlier stores. Not people’s texts — their stock is running out. And not AI’s texts: models that learn from the texts of earlier models in place of human ones lose, generation by generation, what is rare and unusual [29, 30]. The bank holds a third thing — the joint creative work of human and AI: how they worked together, where the person corrected the AI, and what came of it in practice. Half of such raw material is already in use: with people’s consent, researchers collected a million real conversations with a chatbot and fine-tune models on them [31], but how things ended beyond the conversation is not in those records. When open human texts run out, such raw material will become the main one — and it will be supplied by the third shift.

The bank has a duty to protect both the donors and the people their lessons are about. A doctor’s lesson contains a patient, a teacher’s a student, a lawyer’s a client, and then the donor’s consent alone is not enough: some lessons will remain closed. And ideas from which the author wants to earn an income do not go into the bank at all: the bank is a library of experience, not a showcase of inventions. Such an idea belongs in a separate showcase, and only after a patent application has been filed.

## Who will come back to tell how it ended

Getting consent for a finished conversation is easy. Asking a person a month later to recall what they did and how it ended is already work, and work needs a reason: to improve a tool you use every day, to get help with a project of your own, or simply to be paid. So some people give lessons as donors — for thanks and small privileges, the way a blood donor gets a day off and a meal — while a return to an old matter can be commissioned by the bank and paid for as work.

There are two traps here. The success may not have come from the AI’s advice: the person followed it only halfway, or was helped from outside. And those for whom things worked out come back more readily — in which case the bank becomes a collection of success stories, although a failure often teaches more. Both traps can be reduced. The lesson records what the person actually did — fully, halfway or otherwise — and how it is known how things ended. And the bank, with the person’s consent, goes back to unfinished matters and failures itself, rather than waiting for people to volunteer them. The traps will not vanish entirely, but a pencil note saying “in our oven” is worth more than a handsome “it all worked.”

## Why not under the maker’s roof

The organization can also be set up under a maker’s roof — most likely that is how it will all begin: the maker that is first to protect its users and help them secure their ideas will gather people with ideas around it and gain what advertising cannot buy — the reputation of an ally. It is simpler still to build a button into the product: “come back and tell us how it ended.” The technology for it already exists: in the autumn of 2025 one of the largest AI makers showed a mode in which the assistant starts the conversation itself — overnight it goes through past chats, and in the morning it sends a set of updates on what, by its guess, will be useful next. But its description contains no question about how yesterday’s matter ended [32].

But such a button has a limit, and the limit is not technical. People answer the one who gave the advice politely, not honestly: it is awkward to tell a doctor that the treatment did not help. So outcomes, I believe, should be gathered by a hand to which the person owes no politeness. For now this is an assumption, and it can be tested: compare, on similar cases, where people more often report failures — to the maker’s button or to an independent collector; that is why the specification keeps such outcomes separate [15]. Aviation has tested something similar, only there the obstacle was fear, not politeness: in 1975 the U.S. aviation regulator began collecting pilots’ incident reports itself and in six months received not a single one; once collection passed to an independent party that does not punish, more than 1.8 million reports came in over 45 years [33]. Besides, the lesson would stay with one maker, while the person may have asked three AIs at once and chosen among three pieces of advice.

There is a second reason. A hospital has a patient advocate, but the advocate’s salary is paid by the hospital, and a patient who decides to sue it goes to a lawyer of their own. A service under the maker’s roof is the same: in a dispute over shares it is on the side of whoever pays it. In the United States, as early as 1935, a law banned unions created and financed by the employer itself [34]. So there is no need to choose — these are two floors. The lower floor is the user-protection services inside the makers: they compete over who treats the user more fairly. The upper floor is the independent organization: it compares those services, settles disputes and awards a mark — “this maker is fair to its users.”

Where the money comes from. Membership dues can be deducted from the subscription fee, say a dollar a year; makers can also support the society themselves — take it under their patronage. But both the forwarded dues and the patronage count toward that maker’s share, and the share may not exceed a quarter of the budget — which is why the money is collected from several makers and directly from users.

As for scale: at the largest maker, in early 2026, there were about 50 million paying subscribers [35]; if only one in ten agrees to the dues, that is about 5 million dollars a year. Those who use AI for free are admitted without dues: their contribution is mind, and their voice counts no less. Soviet trade unions also had their dues withheld from wages, and that did not make them independent: what decides is not the route the money takes but who governs. Makers may have a seat at the table, but not the deciding vote. And there may be several such societies: more than one chair can stand at the table.

## Why this is good for the maker

This piece is not about a war with the makers. Let me say it plainly: I am in favor of my dialogues with AI, and their results, going into the training of new models, and this text is an example of that. But I want such participation to be properly arranged. The creators of the British collective license present it as legal certainty for AI developers themselves [36]: pay one body and work in peace. It is the same here. As long as the users’ contribution is in the shadow, the maker gets either a free but dwindling source or, as soon as it starts to pay, a flood of fakes. In the light it gets one counterpart instead of a billion, verified lessons instead of invented ones, rules known in advance instead of lawsuits.

The demand for outcomes already shows in the money: vendors of AI for customer support charge not for access but for each request the AI resolves on its own [37]. Yet at one of them a conversation also counts as “resolved” when the person simply asks nothing more: the outcome is measured at the edge of the conversation, and it is counted by the party that is paid for it. What became of the person afterwards, neither seller nor buyer knows. That knowledge is what a lesson bank could bring.

The benefit will have to be shown in practice: the maker has other routes too — hiring experts, commissioning tasks. The bank will interest it if it brings the experience the maker needs, of clear provenance and at a reasonable cost of verification. An AI learns only from what has been loaded into it [38], and outcomes from real life are not being loaded into it today. The industry’s foundation has been built: hardware, software, billions of users. Light on the users’ participation is the next floor.

## Where this could break

Crumbs. Nobody organizes for the sake of a crumb-sized contribution; societies arose where there was something of weight — songs, books. It makes sense to start with professionals: their lessons weigh a great deal, and makers already buy their experience from intermediaries today [4, 39].

Whose money. If the society is fed by one maker, or if one maker alone decides where the dues go, it becomes that maker’s department. Then the chair will be empty again, only with a nameplate on it.

Fear. The most valuable lesson reads: “The AI advised, I acted, it went badly.” For a doctor, a lawyer or an engineer that is an admission against oneself, and without protection such lessons will not come. Aviation gets its reports because they may not be used for penalties and because the collector keeps no record of the author’s name [33]. The lesson bank has no such protection yet; how to build it is an open question of the specification [15].

Verification. Whether lessons really help can be found out in only one honest way — the way a student is tested: on problems the student has not seen. Some of the lessons with known outcomes are withheld during training, and after release the new model is compared with a model that has not seen these lessons: does the new one more often advise what actually worked, and warn against what did not? Guessing the outcome is not enough — the benefit lies in the advice. One can start small: a single community where the outcome can be checked — translators with their clients’ comments, for example — and a single maker willing to try. If the lessons do not help, the design of the bank will have to change: an idea that can be tested may fail the test. But the users’ need for a representative of their own will not go away: contracts, rules and disputes over shares remain whether lessons help or not.

## This essay is itself an example of a lesson

This text grew out of just such a path. The idea of a users’ society came to a user in a conversation with Claude, the AI made by Anthropic; the text was checked by other AIs — ChatGPT, Grok and Mistral. They caught one another’s errors — that is how the claim that the niche was empty was refuted and had to be narrowed — and a human decided. The conversation could have stayed in a log on a server, without a name; instead, who contributed what was written down. Yet by the measure it itself proposes, what you have before you is not yet a lesson but the beginning of one: the task, the moves and the corrections are there, while the outcome — what happens to the text beyond the conversation — still lies ahead. It is from such half-lessons that the AI industry learns today wherever the outcome does not show at once.

## In place of a conclusion

Let us go back to the path. The builders need the people who know where the water stands after rain; the people need a road they can walk without getting their feet wet. Their work is different; the result is shared. The question is not whether makers will offer users a deal: they already pay experts for their experience and have an eye on a share of other people’s successes. Nor is it whether the billions of users will come out of the shadow: if not today, then in ten years the question will be raised anyway. The question is how much will be lost while they, and the shared experience that AI learns from, remain in the shadow — and whether by then there will be anyone to sit in the chair across the table.

The path can already be seen. It does not need to be invented — it needs light: to recognize the users’ contribution now, to see the commercial benefit of acting in time, to give the billions of AI users a legal organization of their own, and to seat it at the negotiating table with the maker.

To tap the potential of this capital today, here and now — not in ten years, once the time has been lost.
