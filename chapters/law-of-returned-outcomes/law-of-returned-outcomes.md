# The Law of Returned Outcomes

*Statement, Scope, Testing. A specification to the essay “Is AGI Really General?”*

Igor Kapustin · written with Claude (Anthropic); reviews, edits, ideas: ChatGPT, Mistral, Gemini · 24 November 2026 · version 1.0 · translated from the Russian original

DOI: [10.5281/zenodo.24503618](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.24503618) · a chapter of The Bush Book · CC BY 4.0

**How to read.** Positions are numbered ZV-01…ZV-64. The word “must” here means: without this position the law or its test does not work; it is a condition, not a demand on anyone. Numbers in square brackets are the numbers of the sources at the end.

## 1. Purpose

**ZV-01.** The Law of Returned Outcomes is the basic law for building tests of AI in everyday affairs (ZV-08): where the result of advice has no quick independent measure and the outcome is visible only to the person it happened to. Such a test is a necessary condition for the transition from computational AI to prognostic AI (ZV-58): it opens the second generation and serves as a school for the third (ZV-54). Why the second generation will not appear by itself without it, and why outcomes must be collected now, is set out in section 12 (ZV-62). The specification states the law so that it can be built on and tested: what it claims, where it applies, what to record in each lesson, and how to refute the law. The pictures and arguments are in the essay “Is AGI Really General?”, published together with this specification [1], and in the working texts — the essay “Better Like This? Or Like This?” and the note “The Law of Returned Outcomes”; the rules for storing lessons and for consent are in the specification “The AI Users’ Society and the Lesson Bank” [2]. They are not repeated here.

**ZV-02.** The specification demands nothing of either AI makers or users. It describes a condition without which, by the law, the usefulness of AI advice in everyday affairs cannot be confirmed, and says how both the law and the author’s supposition can be tested or refuted.

## 2. Definitions

**ZV-03.** **Advice —** an AI answer after which a person decides or does something in his life.

**ZV-04.** **Outcome —** what happened after the advice, as told by the person himself, together with his judgement of whether it is good or bad for him. The judgement cannot be reconstructed from outside: what ought to be does not follow from what is [3]. Two people lose the same job: for one it is a disaster, for the other a long-awaited freedom.

**ZV-05.** **Outcome return —** the account of the outcome that the person brings back: to the same conversation with the AI, to the lesson bank, or to wherever models are trained on such accounts.

**ZV-06.** **Lesson —** a record “goal — situation — advice — decision — what came of it”, with dates. Its parts are described in section 5.

**ZV-07.** **Short loop and long loop.** The short loop is a check that comes at once: whether the next word of a text was guessed correctly in training [4], a rating of the answer at the moment of the conversation (“like”), a check by a program, a proof. The long loop is a check that comes later: the outcome in life. Both are parts of one scheme, “test — operate — test — exit”, described in 1960 by Miller, Galanter and Pribram (TOTE) [5]. The law concerns the second test of the long loop. In everyday language, the short loop asks whether things were done as intended, and the long loop asks, “Is this what I wanted?”; both questions stand side by side in a 2002 self-management handbook [6]. Engineers have known this pair for a long time: checking whether a thing was built right according to its description, and checking whether the right thing was built for what a person needs — after B. Boehm [7]. One can pass the first check and fail the second.

**ZV-08.** **Everyday affairs —** a person’s decisions about his own life or the lives of those close to him, whose rightness cannot be checked at once: only what later happens to him will show it. Five signs: there is no single correct answer for everyone — what is good the person decides himself, by his own goal (ZV-04); the result does not come at once — it comes in weeks, months, years; it is visible only to the person it happened to, and neither a program nor an exam will check it (ZV-22); other people take part in the outcome (ZV-30); the outcome is shaped not only by the advice but also by chance, other people and circumstances. Examples: family and relationships, raising children, work, money, moving house, health and law — when the person decides himself (whether to be treated, how to live with an illness, whether to go to court, whether to sign a contract). The opposite pole is bookish tasks: their rightness is checked at once and in the same way for everyone — to calculate, prove, translate, find a fact. The boundary runs not between subjects but inside the advice: in the question “mortgage or renting?” the calculation is a bookish task, while whether the person will be better off in a year is an everyday affair. The name is a working one (ZV-55).

**ZV-09.** **Light, half-shadow and shadow —** three zones by when the outcome comes: at once (light); late, and only if the person is being followed (half-shadow); never, until the person himself returns it (shadow).

## 3. The law

**ZV-10.** **The law of the goal.** A goal is reached reliably when an answer about the result comes back: whether it succeeded or not. Without such an answer, a system reaches not the goal but the yardstick that was put in its place; engineers call this Goodhart’s law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to measure honestly [8]. This is a general principle of feedback. As early as 1943, Rosenblueth, Wiener and Bigelow singled out purposeful behaviour governed by feedback from the result [9]; from this idea grew cybernetics — the science of how machines, animals and people close the gap between what is and what was intended [10]. The Law of Returned Outcomes is this principle applied to AI advice in everyday affairs; the 1943 paper supports the principle but does not prove the law itself.

**ZV-11.** **The law of the delayed outcome.** Where the choice is made now and the outcome comes later, whoever is being assessed learns to please at the moment of assessment — until someone records the promise and returns the outcome. The law is broader than AI: it works in medicine, in trade and in any new venture.

**ZV-12.** **The law for AI.** 

> Where a matter has an independent measure — a board, a proof, a table — AI can improve and be checked without people. Where there is no such measure, as in people’s everyday affairs — family, relationships, work, raising children, big decisions — without the return of outcomes the growing usefulness of AI advice can be neither confirmed nor told apart from ingratiation; nor can one be sure that the advice does no harm. An outcome is what happened, how it went, whether the person used the machine’s advice, and how he himself judges the result.

The essay “Is AGI Really General?” states the law in plain words: “If advice cannot be checked at once, and only the person knows what it led to, then the usefulness of such advice from a machine cannot be confirmed until people tell how things actually turned out. Until then, advice that helped is indistinguishable from advice that was liked — and there is no way to make sure the advice does no harm.” “Cannot be checked at once” means that the matter has no independent measure; “only the person knows” means that the outcome is visible only to the person it happened to (ZV-08, ZV-22).

**ZV-13.** **The author’s supposition.** “Where there is mathematics, everything is fast and immediate. Where there are people, there will be no AI progress without the return of outcomes.” This prediction is stronger than the law: it claims that without outcomes the usefulness of advice in everyday affairs will not move at all, not merely that it cannot be verified. The test in section 7 can refute it. The essay “Is AGI Really General?” also names the other side of the same supposition: from returned outcomes the machine will learn to see ahead. So far it has only one support — forecasts of events whose outcome is visible to all (ZV-42).

**ZV-14.** **The human’s place in the loop.** The human is already in the AI training loop, but as a rater of answers — a judge of lenses in the optometrist’s chair: the user clicks “like”, hired raters compare the model’s answers and rank them by quality, and the model is further trained on these ratings [4]. The law requires including the human in the loop in a different role — as the one to whom the outcome happened and who returns it. These are two roles of one person: at the optometrist’s, the patient first answers in the chair, “Is this better?”, and a week later comes back to say that he has headaches.

**ZV-15.** **Status.** The law speaks of verification, not of improvement. The parts of advice that have their own measure — calculation, facts, logic — can be improved without outcomes. The usefulness of a whole everyday matter cannot be checked without them: from outside, growing usefulness and growing ingratiation look the same. The return of outcomes is not the only path to improvement, but it is the only judge. And returning alone is not enough to make advice better: the outcomes still have to be analysed (ZV-33). The word “law” in the name is the author’s: C. N. Parkinson in 1955 and A. Clarke in 1962 likewise called ideas from their essays laws [11, 12]. By strict standards it is a principle — a starting proposition and a rule for practice, derived by reasoning, by a thought experiment; the bold part is the author’s supposition about a regularity (ZV-13). Testing will make it a law in the full sense (section 7).

**ZV-16.** **Supports.** The first is a proven theorem: no processing of data can add information that was not in it [13]; so the machine will not learn of an outcome if the outcome never reached it. The second is a present-day fact: as far as is known, nobody systematically collects the outcomes of AI advice in everyday affairs; in 2026 researchers proposed doing so for the first time (ZV-43). The first support can be read in a textbook; the second anyone can check.

## 4. Scope

**ZV-17.** **Where the law applies.** Where the outcome of advice comes later than the conversation and is not checked by the task itself: in everyday affairs (shadow) and in fields where the outcome becomes visible years later, as in medicine (half-shadow).

**ZV-18.** **Where the law is not needed.** Where an answer is checked at once — by a proof, by checking a program, by calculation. There the short loop is enough, and AI grows fast: a model rewarded only for automatically verified correct answers learned on its own to check and revise its solutions [14], and in May 2026 a model disproved a conjecture of Erdős that was nearly eighty years old [15]. Researchers call this the verifier’s law: the easier it is to check a solution to a task, the easier it is to teach AI to solve it [16]. The Law of Returned Outcomes is about the other side of the same idea: where only life checks a decision, the returned outcome becomes the check. Nor is the law needed where the outcome is seen by devices or documents — steps, sleep, money (ZV-37): their readings can be collected without an account. But they will not show “Is this what I wanted?” (ZV-57), and such data about a person and about those his matter concerns may be collected only with their consent.

**ZV-19.** **A long loop without a board.** The AI industry knows long loops where the outcome returns by itself. A chess program learned to play by playing against itself, knowing only the rules; it made corrections from the result of each game [17]. The result of a game is indisputable — a win, a loss or a draw — and visible on the board. In the same way, the outcome of forecasts about events in plain view returns by itself: the news announces it, and models are already being further trained and examined on it (ZV-42). The law concerns a loop that has no board: the outcome is visible only to the person it happened to, it will not return by itself, and a simulated outcome will not replace it. The importance of precisely this loop has not yet been realized.

**ZV-20.** **The shadow has two layers.** The first layer is nature: in life the outcome comes late, and that cannot be changed. The second is how society is arranged: the outcome exists, but nobody collects it — like a diagnosis after which the patient is no longer followed. The law is about the second layer: it can be removed.

**ZV-21.** **What the law does not claim.** 

- That without the return of outcomes AI will not get better: the parts of advice that have their own measure improve without them (ZV-15).
- That the return of outcomes will by itself make AI a good adviser (ZV-15).
- That the “like” rating is useless: it honestly measures the impression at the moment of the answer, but not the outcome.
- That every outcome is caused by the advice: a lesson shows a link, not a cause — those who listen to advice differ from those who do not.
- That people are obliged to return outcomes: returning is voluntary.
- That the very idea of learning from outcomes is discovered here for the first time: others are approaching it independently (ZV-43, ZV-57); what is new in the law is stated in ZV-46.

**ZV-22.** **Who can return an outcome.** Only the person it happened to: an outcome is not only facts but also the person’s own judgement (ZV-04).

## 5. The lesson: what to record

**ZV-23.** **Five parts of a lesson:** the goal — what the person wanted at the moment of the advice and by what signs he himself will see how it turned out; the situation — without identifying details; the gist of the advice; the person’s decision — he used the advice fully, partly, or acted his own way; what came of it — in his own words and with his own judgement.

**ZV-24.** **Goal and expectation — before the outcome.** The goal and the expectation are recorded at the moment of choice; otherwise the judgement gets adjusted after the fact: memory quietly shifts expectations closer to what happened [18]. Medicine has long known how to record an expectation. In goal attainment scaling, proposed in 1968, the expected outcome is described for each goal before treatment, together with four more levels — somewhat better and much better, somewhat worse and much worse; later the level reached is marked [19, 20]. The scale is widely used in rehabilitation. For a lesson this is a ready-made model: each person has his own yardstick, and it is written down before the outcome. In AI training, however, the goal is often not named at all today: learning from human ratings was invented precisely for tasks that are easy to judge by eye but hard to describe [21] — like a client who promises to know what he wants when he sees it [6]. For an everyday matter this shortcut does not work: what is judged by eye is the answer, not what came after it.

**ZV-25.** **In his own words.** The person writes the outcome himself: after a talk with a machine it is easy to retell its view as one’s own [22].

**ZV-26.** **Dates and checkpoints.** A lesson must have two dates — of the advice and of the return. A lesson can be added to at checkpoints — at agreed times after the decision, until the outcome becomes clear. At each checkpoint the person is asked not only “what came of it” but also “is your goal still the same?”; the first record is not rewritten, the new one is placed beside it, with a date. The goal changes along the way: in medicine this is called response shift — a person’s internal standards, his values and his very understanding of what he is assessing change [23]. So a “no” to the question “Is this what I wanted?” means one of two things: he did not get there — or he got there and realized he had wanted something else. AI has its own danger here: if training treats a person’s wishes as fixed, it may reward the machine for shifting them itself in directions the person would not want [24].

**ZV-27.** **Four cells.** Every lesson falls into one of four cells, and all four are recorded in the same way. The most instructive for the machine is “acted his own way, and it turned out well”: there life knew more than the machine. Researchers of trust in AI advice know a similar table: accepting correct advice, rejecting incorrect advice, accepting incorrect advice, rejecting correct advice [25]. But there the correct answer is known in advance. In an everyday matter, only the outcome shows whether the advice was right, so here the cells are filled by returned outcomes, not by an answer key.

|   | Turned out well | Turned out badly |
|---|---|---|
| **Used the AI’s advice** | The advice may have helped | A signal of possible harm |
| **Acted his own way** | The person knew what the machine did not | The advice may have been right — or the matter was hard whatever the choice |

**ZV-28.** **A class of stories teaches.** One account is still a hypothesis: from a single “what came of it” it does not follow that without the advice things would have been worse; good advice can lead to failure, and bad advice to chance success. What teaches is a class of similar stories. A class also gives a second way to measure an outcome. The first is to compare it with the person’s own goal. The second is to compare it with how things went for others in similar matters; in forecasting this is called the outside view: the estimate is built from the actual outcomes of similar past cases, not from the details of one’s own plan [26]. For everyday affairs only a lesson bank can provide such a class. But a class teaches about the class. The average temperature across a hospital says nothing about the patient in ward five. In 2018 researchers checked this on six studies in which the same people were measured many times: the picture for the group did not match the picture of an individual over time. Their conclusion: a finding can be carried over from a group to a person only after checking that the process works the same way in the group and in the person, and human processes are usually individual and change over time [27]. So next to the class one needs the person’s own series of lessons — his advice and outcomes in various matters over time. The class suggests what usually happens to similar people, the series what happens to this person; the series stays with the person (ZV-30), the class is gathered by the bank.

**ZV-29.** **Failure is a lesson too.** “Did not help” and “did harm” are recorded on a par with successes. But one cannot wait for reports: few come back on their own, and the accounts of those who return on their own do not show how things went for the rest. When people are asked, however, most answer: in the British study, about 88% (ZV-44). How little voluntary reporting yields is visible in medicine: even for adverse drug reactions, for which reporting systems exist, in half of 37 studies 94% of cases or more never reached the system [28]. These are reports to a reporting system, not accounts to a conversation partner, but the lesson is the same. So the outcome is asked about at checkpoints, without waiting.

**ZV-30.** **The original stays with the person.** What goes out is a lesson with identifying details removed; what to give and for what use — in this conversation, in the lesson bank, in training future models — the person decides himself. Other people almost always take part in an outcome, and they have not given consent. The rules for storage and consent are in the specification “The AI Users’ Society and the Lesson Bank” [2].

## 6. What the parties can do

**ZV-31.** **To an AI maker the law suggests five steps.** 

- Before advice in an everyday matter, ask about the goal and the expectation — what should change and by when — and state a forecast (ZV-49). Dissatisfaction without a stated expectation teaches whoever does the work to guess: in one everyday story, the workmen kept guessing how to please the owner of a renovation, while he was waiting for a stripe on the wall not far below the ceiling and had not said so beforehand [6].
- Separate the judgement of the moment from the outcome, and do not train the model on “like” alone: such a reward can teach a model to please — one update already had to be rolled back because of flattery [29].
- Provide for a second visit: with the person’s consent, after a set time, remind him of his question and briefly ask what came of it (ZV-52).
- Count a lesson in full, in all four cells, not only the successes.
- Do not count silence as an outcome: in one AI customer-support service, a conversation counts as “resolved” even when the customer simply did not ask for help again [30].

**ZV-32.** **An independent collector.** The collector of outcomes must be independent of whoever releases the models and is paid for their work; otherwise the outcomes are defined by someone interested in good numbers. A model for such a collector already exists: since 15 April 1976 aviation has had an incident reporting system; it was designed to be voluntary, confidential, non-punitive and independent, and was entrusted to a neutral third party [31]. A model for funding is a consumer organization that lives on subscriptions and itself buys everything it tests [32]. The AI Users’ Society could become such a collector [2].

**ZV-33.** **Analysis.** Between the return of an outcome and the correction of advice stands analysis: someone decides where the error was — in the advice, in its execution or in the circumstances. In the aviation system, reports are analysed by specialists, who remove the identifying details of those involved and pass warnings to those who can fix the problem [31, 33]. If two lessons with the same advice gave opposite outcomes, the circumstances are compared first, and only then is the advice judged. This is how evidence-based medicine works. Under the rules of GRADE — an international system for rating how far medical evidence can be trusted — inconsistency between studies is checked against a few explanations named in advance: differences in patients (for example, severity of illness), in treatment (dose, concomitant treatment), in outcomes (length of follow-up) and in methods; if a large inconsistency remains unexplained, confidence in the conclusion is lowered [34, 35]. For lessons these are four questions: how did the people and their circumstances differ; what exactly was done and what else was done at the same time; what was counted as the outcome and when was it asked about; was the goal named before the advice. The explanations are named in advance for the same reason the goal is recorded in advance (ZV-24): differences noticed after the fact may be spurious [34]. Advice with an unexplained inconsistency is declared neither good nor bad — it is marked as unclear. A bank without analysis is just an archive. Who analyses the lessons — the developer, the Users’ Society or both together — is set by the bank’s rules.

**ZV-34.** **To the user** the law suggests four steps — until the machine does them itself (ZV-49, ZV-52): name the goal before the question — what should change and by when; ask for a forecast before deciding — along three paths: act, don’t act, wait (ZV-49) — what usually happens after such a step, what might go wrong, whom else it will affect; after a week or a month, come back to the same conversation and tell what you wanted, what you did and how it ended; keep the last word for yourself — “Is this what I wanted?” is for the person to decide. A general model will not be retrained by one account: training future models is separate work for the developers. A “like” rates the frames, not the eyesight.

**ZV-35.** **A lesson is a gift.** Only the work around it is paid for: keeping records, coming back at checkpoints, removing other people’s identifying details. Payment per piece attracts invented and machine-made lessons: when people were paid for each text summary, between a third and almost half of them handed the work over to AI [36].

## 7. Testing and refutation

**ZV-36.** **Two groups.** The author’s supposition (ZV-13) — that the return of outcomes helps give better advice — is tested by comparing two groups of people who seek advice on everyday affairs, each with its own model. Model A learns from the judgement of the moment (“like”), model B from returned lessons. Everything else is the same for both: the base model, the kind of conversation, the topics.

**ZV-37.** **What is measured.** The goal and the time points are set in advance; at those time points — for example, after three and six months — participants are asked how the matters they sought advice on turned out and how they judge the result. The main measures are the person’s own judgement of the result and the share of the cell “used the advice — it turned out badly”, that is, of signals of harm. One looks not only at the group average but also at each participant’s outcomes across several matters: an average can hide that model B helped some people and harmed others (ZV-28). Such long comparisons have already been run: in two equivalent randomized trials, people worked for ten months with a human coach or with a coaching chatbot, and the attainment of their goals was measured over the whole period [37]. Only a comparison by lot — randomization — separates the merit of the advice from the merit of the person. This is how researchers checked whether an algorithm’s recommendations help judges decide on cash bail: the decisions did not become more accurate, and the algorithm without the judge did worse [38]. The gain may also be modest: in a trial with 13,463 fitness-tracker users, prompts chosen by a learning algorithm produced, after a month, 296 more steps a day than no prompts; after two months the advantage over the no-prompt group held, but no longer over other ways of prompting [39].

**ZV-38.** **Criteria — in advance.** The criterion of confirmation and the criterion of refutation are written down before data collection: what difference between the groups counts as noticeable, and at what result the supposition counts as refuted. If there is no difference, or model B is worse, the supposition has not been confirmed, and this must be honestly admitted. The law itself (ZV-12) would be refuted by someone who shows how, without returned outcomes, to make sure that the chosen measure — a like, an expert’s rating, a test — is linked to what became of people. Nor is the law refuted by a stand-in user — another machine imitating people: to learn whether it imitates them correctly, its answers would have to be checked against the answers of real people.

**ZV-39.** **Freezing the protocol.** The test protocol is frozen on Zenodo before data collection, so that anyone, not only the author, can run the test, and so that the criteria cannot be fitted to the result.

**ZV-40.** **“Do no harm” in the test itself.** Both models give advice with the usual safety measures, and no participant receives knowingly worse advice than he would receive today. Medical ethics works the same way: a new treatment is compared with the best proven one, and placebo is allowed only in special cases — above all when no proven treatment exists [40].

**ZV-41.** **Difficulties.** The outcome depends on more than the advice, so many participants are needed. People forget to come back, so reminders are needed. Different matters take different times to reach an outcome, so checkpoints are chosen by kind of advice.

**ZV-42.** **Early signs that do not replace the test.** 

- If a rater is first shown the consequences of advice, even simulated ones, the model ingratiates less and helps people reach their goals better [41].
- Ratings right after the answer and after real consequences diverge — but that is eight people and two weeks [42].
- One AI maker, having rolled back a flattering update, announced that it was changing how it uses feedback and learning to measure usefulness over the long term, not only by whether an answer was liked in the moment [43].
- Models trained on the realized outcomes of public events forecast noticeably more accurately [44, 45]. On an open exam where forecasts about such events are checked against what happened, several systems, according to its authors in July 2026, have drawn level with the best human forecasters — with the caveat that the human forecasts were collected in 2024 [46]. This supports the author’s supposition (ZV-13), but not the law: such events have a board (ZV-19).
- Medicine has independently come to the same idea for its own field: data become knowledge only when the patient’s condition, the treatment and what became of the patient afterwards are linked [47].

## 8. Neighbours and predecessors

**ZV-43.** **The closest neighbour.** In 2026, a position paper by four researchers from Toronto and Purdue appeared at the main machine learning conference: train language models for people’s well-being rather than for approval in the moment [48]. They propose asking people voluntarily, days and weeks after a conversation, about clarity, regret and progress toward their goals, and using the answers first for evaluating models and, where the method proves itself, for training as well. Of everything we could find, this is the closest to the law.

**ZV-44.** **A ladder.** The search is climbing a single ladder. The first rung is the judgement of the moment (“like”). The second is simulated consequences: the rater is shown where the advice will probably lead (ZV-42), or the model plays the conversation several turns ahead and is rewarded for usefulness at its end [49]. The third is a voluntary survey weeks later (ZV-43): this is how British researchers learned that, of the participants who received advice from chatbots on health, work and relationships, 75–79% of those who answered said they had followed it (there were 6,474 participants in all, and about 88% answered after two to three weeks), but overall those who received advice were no better off than those who chatted with the bot about hobbies [50]. The result was similar in a study of couples’ disagreements: two weeks later, people were better off both after a conversation with a chatbot and after a writing exercise, and on most measures there was no difference between the two [51]. The fourth rung is the real outcome next to a goal recorded before the advice; this is where the law stands.

**ZV-45.** **The loop beyond release.** The EU Artificial Intelligence Act requires the provider of a high-risk system to collect and analyse experience of its use after release [52]. This is a loop extended beyond hand-over. But what is collected there is information about how the system itself works, not about what became of a person after advice, and it applies to high-risk systems, not to everyday advice.

**ZV-46.** **What the law adds.** Five things the neighbours do not yet have: a claim about verification — where there is no measure, without outcomes usefulness can be neither confirmed nor told apart from ingratiation (ZV-12); a goal and an expectation recorded before the advice (ZV-24); four cells, including “acted his own way” (ZV-27); an independent collector and analysis (ZV-32, ZV-33); a consequence for the second generation — without the return of outcomes it can be neither recognized nor built on purpose, and the time outcomes need cannot be bought later (ZV-62). The 2026 position paper has neither a record of the goal before the advice, nor a split into “followed the advice or not”, nor an independent store of outcomes [48]. The idea of learning from outcomes is not new in itself: others are already on this path (ZV-43, ZV-57). But the law in this form — with the claim about verification and its consequence for the second generation — is not found in the works we located.

**ZV-47.** **A predecessor of the lesson bank.** AI has long known how to learn from similar cases. In 1994 the cycle of case-based reasoning — the name for solving a new problem by the most similar old cases — was described in four steps: retrieve similar cases, reuse their solution, revise and correct it, retain the useful experience for the future [53]. The method itself was largely inspired by the study of human memory [54]. Even then the authors wrote that checking usually happens outside the system — the solution is applied in a real case; that its result may not come at once — in medicine the success or failure of a treatment shows after a few hours, or even several months; and that failures can be retained too [53]. So more than thirty years ago both the long loop (ZV-07) and the lesson from failure (ZV-29) were described. The lesson bank is the same cycle for everyday affairs. Two things are new in it: the cases are the lives of many people, and the check is made by an outcome that only the person himself can return (ZV-22).

## 9. The pair and the second visit

**ZV-48.** **The frontal lobe of the pair.** The neuropsychologist A. R. Luria described the brain as three blocks: the first maintains alertness; the second receives, processes and stores information; the third — the frontal lobes — sets the goal, builds a programme, monitors its execution and compares the result obtained with the original task [55, 56]. When the third block is damaged, a person keeps his knowledge and skills but can no longer apply them to a goal [57]. In everyday affairs AI is a huge second block, while the human sets the goal and compares the result: he is the frontal lobe of the pair. J. Licklider distributed the roles the same way back in 1960: the human sets the goals and evaluates the result, the machine does the routine work [58]. The outcome return (ZV-05) is the frontal lobe’s answer passed on to the second block; without it the pair works like a brain in which the frontal lobes compare the result with the goal, but the knowledge block never learns of it. The “test — operate — test” scheme (ZV-07) and the work of the frontal lobes are one idea seen from outside and from inside: one of the scheme’s authors, K. Pribram, edited a book on the frontal lobes together with Luria [59]. This is an image, not anatomy: Luria’s scheme is a working model, and the frontal syndrome he described could not be found in studies of groups of patients [60].

**ZV-49.** **A forecast before the advice.** A good doctor, before prescribing, asks what the patient wants, says what to expect from treatment and how it might harm, schedules a follow-up visit and leaves the decision to the patient. Advice in an everyday matter follows the same order: goal and expectation (ZV-24) — forecast — advice — the person’s decision — second visit. A forecast before the advice answers three questions: what usually happens after such a step; what might go wrong; whom else it will affect (ZV-50). It is most convenient to give it along three paths — act, don’t act, wait — as a doctor chooses between “treat”, “don’t treat” and “watch”; only outcomes put numbers on such paths (ZV-62). “Do no harm” here is not a prohibition but an order of thought: first the consequences, then the advice. The forecast is written into the lesson next to the goal (ZV-23): then at the second visit the result is compared not only with the person’s goal but also with the machine’s forecast, and one can see whether its forecasts come true (ZV-54).

**ZV-50.** **The second person affected and the circles of “do no harm”.** Other people almost always take part in an outcome (ZV-30), while the machine hears one side and readily takes it. When the same quarrel was retold to models now in the words of one side, now in the words of the other, in 48% of cases the models told both sides “you are not to blame” [61]. In experiments with 2,405 participants, after agreeable answers people grew more convinced they were right and less willing to make up, yet they rated such answers higher and trusted such a machine more [62]. The other side may not even know that a machine took part in the matter [63]. It is sharpest where a relative talks to the machine and the one to be treated is a child or an elderly person: models follow the stated wish and pay little attention to whose wish it is and who will bear the consequences; even when they complied, they asked about the patient’s own wishes, or advised finding them out, in only 12.6% of cases [64]. That is why the forecast before the advice contains the question “whom else will this affect?” (ZV-49); the outcome for the other side is also written into the lesson — in the person’s words and, where the relationship allows and there is consent, in that side’s own words; a second visit can be made to them too. The price of ingratiating advice is also paid by someone who was not in the conversation. The circle of “do no harm” widens: the person himself — those close to him — everyone the result will affect — society. The picture is the antibiotic: a doctor does not prescribe it for a cold even if the patient asks — colds are usually caused by viruses, on which antibiotics have no effect [65], and unneeded antibiotics are one of the main causes of microbial resistance, which hits everyone [66]. The outer circles learn only from their own outcomes: the machine will learn how advice echoes in others only if their outcomes come back too — with their consent. Seeing harm to others does not yet mean deciding for the person: the machine shows whom a step will affect and leaves the choice to him; it may refuse only when the harm to others is obvious and serious — like the doctor with the antibiotic.

**ZV-51.** **A crutch or an adaptogen.** The word “adaptogen” was introduced in 1947 by the pharmacologist N. V. Lazarev for substances that raise the body’s non-specific resistance [67]: an adaptogen does not work in place of the body but strengthens the body itself. AI should be an adaptogen for the pair’s frontal lobe, not a crutch: with a crutch the pair is stronger and the person weaker, and at the machine’s first failure he turns out weaker than he was before it. The risk is not made up: researchers list among long-term risks the loss of skills from over-reliance on AI and note that after a run of successes people stop checking the machine’s answers [68]. But this can be fixed: in a study with 704 adults, those who after each hint were told what it meant and asked how much of the next task they would do themselves asked for ready-made answers less often and did better on a test without help [69]. Hence three measures of the value of AI in the pair: what the pair can do; what remains with the person when the machine is switched off; how things turned out for those affected by the result (ZV-50). All three are visible only at the second visit (ZV-31).

**ZV-52.** **A question from the assistant’s memory.** The second visit (ZV-31) can be started by the assistant itself. It remembers the person’s question and its date and, at the agreed time — two weeks later, a month later — comes back to it on its own: “A month ago you asked how to make peace with your brother. How did it go? Do you need any more help?” The person answers in his own words (ZV-25): it worked, it didn’t, it turned out differently, the goal changed (ZV-26). This is not a “like” and not a “yes — no” right after the answer, but an account given once the outcome exists. Consent, timing and topics are set by the person himself, and not answering is his right; silence does not count as an outcome (ZV-31). Technically almost everything for this exists: assistants can write at a scheduled time [70], and one of them sent a digest every morning, compiled from what it remembered about the person and his past conversations [71]. But it learned from thumbs-up and thumbs-down buttons [72]; a reviewer left it to readers to decide whether this was bait for conversations [73]; and the maker itself, when winding the feature down, concluded that such messages are most useful when they are personal, lead to action, and are controlled by the person himself [74]. Hence five dangers of the question “How did it turn out?”: a retention hook instead of a question asked for the sake of the outcome; a leading question (“it got better, didn’t it?”) that suggests the answer; a “success” checkbox instead of an account — the same “like”, only delayed (ZV-25); a judge interested in good numbers (ZV-32); delayed sycophancy — memory embellishes what the person chose himself [75], and a model trained further on such accounts will learn advice that is pleasant to report on. Against it: ask about events, not about judgements; record the forecast before the outcome (ZV-49); compare by lot (ZV-37); and, where there is an independent sign, check the account against it.

**ZV-53.** **A common collection.** An answer left in one conversation teaches only that conversation (ZV-34); scattered across billions of separate conversations, such experience stays fragmented. Knowledge of “where to” is built from many similar paths whose ends are known, so answers to the question “How did it turn out?” — with the person’s consent and without identifying details (ZV-30) — are collected together: in a lesson bank [2], with an independent collector (ZV-32). There they are analysed (ZV-33) and sorted into classes of similar stories, while the person’s own series of lessons stays with him (ZV-28).

**ZV-54.** **Generations and their signs.** The scale is the same as in “The AI Daisy” [76]: “do no harm” grows stronger from generation to generation. The first generation is the calculator: it computes for the sake of computing, agrees with the user and learns from approval and tests; the second lets no error through and learns from returned outcomes; the third is the navigator: before giving advice it plays out a step, sees where it will lead and whom it will affect (ZV-50), together with the person chooses the step that will do no harm in its foreseeable consequences, and leaves the wheel to the person. The picture is a doctor’s path: straight-A student, resident, experienced doctor who gives a forecast before prescribing. The calculator grows in power, prognostic AI in generations. Definitions of general AI measure abilities with tests given to people [77] and do not require release to people [78, 79]; what became of a person after advice is not part of them. That is why two stages of one path are confused under the name of general AI (ZV-58). Computational AI is a straight-A student in all subjects at once and the forerunner of prognostic AI: strong where an answer is checked in minutes, but short-sighted in everyday affairs; on this scale, however many subjects it passes, it is the first generation. Prognostic AI is broad in its own way — not across subjects but across the person — and resembles a general practitioner: it sees the whole person — daily life, money, health and law together — brings the advice together, and knows what became of the person after the advice; harm here is often born at the seams, as with an elderly patient for whom each specialist correctly prescribed his own drug, while together the pills interfere with one another. Medicine works this way too: in Norway, where everyone has a regular family doctor, data on 4.5 million people show that the longer a person stays with one doctor, the less often he needs out-of-hours care and acute hospitalization; the study is observational, but the authors consider the link causal [80]. For everyday affairs the signs are different. The second generation is one whose loop is complete (goal — advice — what came of it — comparison with the goal); it is recognized by its result: on new cases not seen in training, its advice, judged by returned outcomes, does harm less often than that of the first. The third is recognized when forecasts written down before the advice (ZV-49) come true on new cases more often than with the second. It takes shape when outcomes return no longer by the millions but by the billions and accumulate into a library of lived paths (ZV-53); but only full and honest loops will make up the library — billions of likes remain the same pile of ratings, only thicker. The more full loops have returned, the more accurate the forecast; but it is checked only on new cases: what was learned on earlier ones may keep firing where the world has already changed.

## 10. The term and neighbours working on the same problem

**ZV-55.** **The word “everyday”.** The earlier name, “vital affairs” (Russian “zhiznennye dela”), was rejected: thinking is polar, and that word drags in a heavy second pole — ultimate questions where life itself is at stake. In Russian, “zhiznenny” means “important for life”, while “zhiteisky” refers to everyday life [81]; the second pole of “zhiteisky” is the bookish, the scientific. This pair has long worked in Russian psychology: L. S. Vygotsky distinguished everyday concepts, behind which stands what a person has seen and done, from scientific concepts, which bring a subject into a system of verbal definitions [82, 83]. The German Lebensweisheit in the title of A. Schopenhauer’s work is rendered in Russian as “zhiteiskaya mudrost” — everyday wisdom [84]. The risk of the word is a shade of triviality (the Russian “delo zhiteiskoye” means “it happens, no big deal”), so it is given as a pair: bookish tasks — everyday affairs. The name is a working one and may change. (In this English version: “everyday affairs”.)

**ZV-56.** **Predecessors in meaning.** Aristotle called the skill of managing one’s own affairs practical wisdom — phronesis — and remarked that young men become geometers and mathematicians, but a young man of practical wisdom is not found; it deals with particulars, which become known from experience, and experience takes time [85]. The straight-A student without residency is already described in his work. The Latin name of the same wisdom, prudentia, was traced back to providens — “seeing ahead” [86], and Thomas Aquinas listed among its parts memory, foresight, attention to circumstances and caution [87]: that is almost the whole forecast before the advice (ZV-49).

**ZV-57.** **Neighbours working on the same problem.** R. Hogarth described “wicked” learning environments, where feedback on an action is scarce, misleading or absent altogether, and “kind” ones, where feedback links the outcome to the action and is accurate and plentiful [88]; his example is the weather forecaster, who learns every day whether the forecast came true, and the emergency-room doctor, to whom only the patient’s immediate response comes back [89]. This is a predecessor of our “doctor who sees each patient only once”. In 1973 H. Rittel and M. Webber called “wicked” those problems whose solutions have neither an immediate nor an ultimate test, unlike the “tame” problems of mathematics and chess [90]. D. Silver and R. Sutton propose training AI on rewards from consequences in the world rather than from ratings given by people in advance: a health assistant looks at whether sleep has improved, and the reward can also be given by the person himself — saying how tired he is after exercise or how bad his headache is [91]. This is the closest to our law. The difference is that the law speaks not of how to train but of what the usefulness of advice cannot be checked without, and draws from this that the second generation will not appear by itself (ZV-62); and “Is this what I wanted?” cannot be measured by a sensor (ZV-22). Medicine has long recognized that some consequences of illness and treatment are known only to the patients themselves: this is the basis of the questionnaires that patients fill in themselves in drug trials [92]. This is a direct predecessor of the condition “only the person knows what the advice led to”. S. Russell gives the machine a single goal — to fulfil human wishes, which it does not know at first and infers from behaviour [93]; the law proposes not inferring but asking how it turned out. A coherence-aware measure of general AI requires sufficient strength in all important areas without a catastrophic failure in any [94] — this is close to “do no harm”, but inside tests. Commentators also write that a passed bar or medical exam guarantees little about work in messy real life [95].

## 11. Two stages: computational and prognostic AI

**ZV-58.** **Two stages of one path.** Two stages of one path are confused under the name “general AI”; in the essay “Is AGI Really General?” they are called AGI‑1 and AGI‑2 (ZV-61). Computational AI solves bookish tasks (ZV-08): its question is “what will come out if the formula is written this way?”, and the answer is checked at once and in the same way for everyone; it learns from tests and from checks made by a program [96], and it grows in power. Prognostic AI helps in everyday affairs — household, money, medical, legal, educational matters, relations between people: its question is “what will happen later if I do this today?”; it is checked by what later became of the person (ZV-05); it learns from returned outcomes and grows in generations, and the measure of growth is one — “do no harm” (ZV-54), while the circle of “do no harm” widens from the person himself to society (ZV-50). These are not two kinds but a straight-A student and a doctor: computational AI is the forerunner of prognostic AI and its foundation. A stage is defined not by its design but by what it is checked by: both compute, and today it is one and the same machine — checked in bookish tasks, unchecked in everyday affairs (ZV-12). The forerunner is a foundation but not a mere preliminary stage; three caveats make this precise. First: computational AI will not become prognostic by itself — a straight-A student does not become a doctor by passing ever more exams, and without returned outcomes even the most powerful calculator remains the first generation. Second: it does not dissolve into prognostic AI — where the world answers by itself (science, programs, calculations), it is needed in its own right; the trunk is one, but it grows in two directions. Third: there is no need to wait for it to be completed — the outcome of everyday advice ripens over weeks and months, this time cannot be bought like computing power, so outcome collection starts on today’s models (ZV-62): the library of lived paths (ZV-53) grows at the speed of life, not of computers. This has already happened with the weather (ZV-60). The confusion is that the first stage shows the power, while help in life is expected from the second, and brilliance at tasks is mistaken for reliability in everyday affairs. People already come to the machine for advice: in an analysis of 1.5 million conversations with a chatbot that has 700 million weekly users, about 70% of messages are not work-related, and almost half of all messages are requests to find something out or to get advice [97]. The second stage is prepared by a question from the assistant’s memory (ZV-52) and by a common collection (ZV-53), with the outcomes of both those who followed the advice and those who did not (ZV-27).

**ZV-59.** **The word “prognostic” and the limit of forecasting.** The word “general” is left to the computational stage: it is about how many subjects have been passed. The stage the law is about is called prognostic — after the medical pair “diagnosis — prognosis”: a diagnosis says what is there now, a prognosis what will happen if one acts this way or that. In the industry the word is taken by another meaning. Predictive AI — in Russian “prediktivny” or “prognostichesky II” — means algorithms that use accumulated data to predict what will happen next, for example which customers will soon cancel a subscription [98]. The researchers A. Narayanan and S. Kapoor use the term for systems that predict people’s futures so that others can decide for them — whether a defendant will commit a new crime, whether a job applicant will cope with the work — and show that such systems often do not work: predicting the future is hard, and AI does not change that [99, 100, 101]. Prognostic AI in the sense of the law is marked by three signs: the forecast is given to the person himself; it is about his own step (“if I do this”), not about him; the person decides. Economists divide the work the same way: the machine makes prediction cheaper, while judgement — what to do with the prediction — stays with the human, and its value grows [102]. Hence a prohibition: the library of lived paths (ZV-53) must not be turned into predictions about people for a bank, an employer or a court. And a limit: in a competition of 160 scientific teams, predictions of six outcomes in the lives of teenagers and their families, built from data from birth to age nine, were inaccurate even for the best and only slightly better than a simple model with four features [103, 104]. The navigator is not an oracle: it says what usually happens after such a step and where the forecast is weak, and proposes a step that is easy to undo (ZV-49); its forecasts are checked against returned outcomes (ZV-54).

**ZV-60.** **A precedent: the calculator that became a weather forecaster.** The ENIAC computer was built above all to calculate artillery tables [105]; in 1950 the first computer weather forecast was made on it — a 24-hour forecast took 24 hours to compute, and the results, far from perfect, justified continuing the work [106]. The calculator took decades to become a forecaster: computers, the physics in the models and observational data all grew, and the forecast was checked every day against what happened — and over forty years it lengthened by about a day per decade [107]. Checking teaches not only accuracy but also honesty: forecasters can give reliable probabilistic forecasts of precipitation and temperature — the stated probability comes true on average [108], and feedback on past forecasts, in an experiment in 1981–1982, slightly improved both the reliability and the skill of probabilistic forecasts [109]. This is exactly what the navigator needs: to say where the forecast is weak (ZV-59). A precedent is not a copy; there are two differences. First: the weather answers the next day and by itself, while an everyday outcome answers after weeks and months and only through the person (ZV-22, ZV-52). Second: a forecaster’s forecast does not change the weather, while advice changes the life it predicts — like a doctor who acts on his own diagnosis [89]; such predictions are called performative: they affect what they predict [110]. That is why an everyday forecast needs not only the outcome but also whether the person followed the advice — the outcomes of both those who followed it and those who did not (ZV-27).

## 12. Why outcomes must be collected now

**ZV-61.** **AGI‑1 and AGI‑2.** This is what the essay “Is AGI Really General?” calls the two stages of one path (ZV-58). The essay’s title asks whether a machine recognized as general by exams will be general in the human sense. The answer: by abilities — yes; whether it sees ahead what its advice will lead to cannot be learned from exams — only the second scale will show that (ZV-63). AGI‑1 is a machine that passes all the exams by which general AI is measured today [77, 78]: the computational stage, on the scale of generations — the first (ZV-54). AGI‑2 is the prognostic stage: a machine that can also foresee what its advice will lead to; on the same scale — the second generation. These are not two kinds of AI but the development of one: the first is the foundation of the second. The first will show its power, but it is the second that people expect help from in their own affairs.

**ZV-62.** **AGI‑2 will not appear by itself.** As long as outcomes are not collected, the second generation cannot be brought closer. There are three reasons.

- It cannot be recognized. The second generation is recognized by its result: on new cases its advice does harm less often and helps more often (ZV-54). Neither exams nor the “like” rating will show this: definitions of general AI do not include what became of a person after advice [77, 78], and a “like” is given before the outcome, and more readily for agreement [62, 111]. Without the return of outcomes, AGI‑2 cannot be told apart from AGI‑1 (ZV-12).
- It cannot be built on purpose. What gets improved is what can be measured; without an answer about the outcome, a system reaches not the goal but the yardstick (ZV-10) — today that means exams and “like”. The path along them leads to the straight-A student and to sycophancy. The market will not help either: sales, subscriptions and time in the app are the same “like”, only in money; the market will show what people want, but not whether the advice helped. The machine can also be taught on other people’s stories in which it is already known what a decision led to — but that is someone else’s experience, not the outcomes of its own advice: whether the skill carried over to its own advice will be shown only by the outcomes of that advice. Even if along the way the machine starts advising better, there will be nothing to check it with: advice in an everyday matter has one judge — the returned outcome (ZV-15).
- It cannot be bought later. Outcomes accumulate at the speed of life, not of computers (ZV-58). Medicine knows this: to compare three paths for a malignant prostate tumour that has not yet spread — to monitor, to operate, to irradiate — British researchers, from 1999 to 2009, with the patients’ consent, assigned one of the three paths by lot to 1,643 men and followed them for 11 to 21 years; the fifteen-year results came out in 2023: survival hardly depended on the path chosen, but under monitoring the tumour spread more often [112], while after surgery and radiotherapy complications affecting quality of life remained more often [113]. So now the patient chooses together with the doctor, weighing what matters more to him. Had the collection not started then, no amount of money could have produced these numbers later. Advice in a family quarrel echoes for years — in the life of the one who asked and in the lives of his children — while the largest study of chatbot advice we found asked people after two to three weeks (ZV-44). And collecting outcomes once is not enough: people and circumstances change, and old outcomes go out of date — the collection must be continuous. Every year without collecting outcomes pushes the second generation further away.

**Status.** The first two steps are consequences of the law for AI (ZV-12) and of the law of the goal (ZV-10); the third is an observation about time. Stronger than this is the author’s supposition (ZV-13): that without outcomes the usefulness of advice itself will not move, and that from outcomes the machine will learn to see ahead.

**ZV-63.** **The second scale.** Next to the exam scale a second one is needed — based on returned outcomes (ZV-53): how often, after the machine’s advice, things turned out as the person wanted; how often they turned out badly — the share of the cell “used the advice — it turned out badly” (ZV-27); how often the forecast recorded before the outcome came true (ZV-49). A forecast that came true and the usefulness of the advice are different questions: the forecast might have come true without the advice; usefulness is shown by comparison. Comparing those who followed the advice with those who declined it is not enough: these people differ even without the advice; a comparison by lot is reliable — between reasonable pieces of advice, or between the machine’s advice and the usual practice, with people’s consent and without knowingly worse advice (ZV-37, ZV-40). The scale is computed on new and fresh cases — a new model learns from the outcomes returned to earlier models, and old outcomes, moreover, go out of date — from the answers of both those who followed the advice and those who did not, and it is kept by an independent collector (ZV-32). This is not a new section of the exam but a scale of a different kind: an exam is graded on the day it is taken, while here the answer is given by the person himself, and it comes later. Exams are the machine’s diploma; the second scale is what became of people after its advice: AGI‑1 is recognized by exams, AGI‑2 by the second scale. It cannot be filled in before meeting people: it is accumulated in the course of work, like a residency. Even then the decision stays with the person: the scale will show how far the machine can be trusted, but the choice and the responsibility are his; he is the one who signs (ZV-49). For forecasts about events in plain view such an exam already exists (ZV-42); for everyday affairs it does not. The second scale can be started on today’s models — with the question “How did it turn out?” from the assistant’s memory (ZV-52), asked at checkpoints (ZV-26): a big matter has more than one answer — about an act one asks after days, about a storyline after months, about an area of life after years.

**ZV-64.** **Slices and movement over time.** A single overall number on the second scale (ZV-63) hides the main thing: where the machine already sees ahead and where it does not yet. So the scale is also counted by slices — by area of affairs, age, country, climate, season — and over different time spans: the same advice may help the young and not the old, serve well in summer and let one down in winter, look successful after a month and unsuccessful after a year. This is how weather forecasters check forecasts against the weather separately for each region and each season, and know where a forecast can be relied on (ZV-60). Slices checked year after year give three things. A map of skill: where forecasts recorded before the advice (ZV-49) come true more and more often, and where they do not — what a navigator must say about the weakness of a forecast (ZV-59). An early warning: if after a change of model the outcomes in some slice have become worse, that is a signal of possible harm — this is how the side effects of a medicine come to light, from reports collected after it has gone on sale. A shelf life for lessons: one can see when earlier outcomes stop coming true and lessons go stale. The limits are those of a class of stories (ZV-28): a slice speaks of the slice, not of the person, and “people your age usually…” must not become a label. Slices can give a person away even without a name: in the 1990 US census, for 87% of residents the combination of ZIP code, gender and date of birth was most likely shared with no one else [114]; a recount on the 2000 census gave 63% [115]. So only large slices go out, and small ones, with few stories, are not shown. A slice is honest only if everyone was asked about the outcome, rather than waiting to see who comes back on their own (ZV-29). And yearly series cannot be bought after the fact: the earlier collection begins, the sooner the movement becomes visible (ZV-62).
